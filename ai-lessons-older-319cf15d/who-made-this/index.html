Who Made This?

Students name the style of three AI versions of the same photo, try a Visual Code on a photo of an object, and work out who made the result and when to say that AI was used.

At a glance

Ages
16–19

Groups

Students use AI
Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, name the style: one per student, printed in colour if you can, or in black and white with the pictures shown in colour on the projector
  • Page 2, who made this? and two closing-question tickets: one per pair
  • One device per pair with a camera and the AI tool your school has approved, if the tool can edit pictures
  • A projector, for the Visual Codes page if students work without AI
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Describe a visual style in precise words, and explain that an image model learned its styles from pictures that people made.
  • Judge, for six cases, how much of a piece of work a person made and how much AI made.
  • Explain when to say that AI was used, and why the rules on who owns AI-made work differ between countries.

Key words

style
The way something is made: colours, lines, what is shown and what is left out.
Visual Code
A one-word prompt, such as /cutaway, that an image tool turns into a whole style when you send it with a photo. Collected by Choosewise.
copyright
The right of the person who made a work to control who may copy, share or sell it.
deepfake
A picture, sound or video made or changed with AI that looks real and could be taken for a real person, place or event.
disclose
Say openly that AI was used, and how.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print page 1 for every student and page 2 for every pair. Students can write on page 1 in black and white, but the styles are hard to tell apart without colour: print it in colour if you can, or show the pictures on the projector while students write.
  • Check whether the AI tool your school has approved can edit an uploaded photo, and that it is approved for students' age. If it can't, or students can't log in, run step 4 without AI on the Visual Codes page.
  • Open choosewise.education/visual-codes/ before class and pick three cards to show if you need the path without AI.
  • Tell students before step 4: photos of objects only, never a person, themselves included. A photo of a person is personal data, and AI can turn it into something that person never agreed to.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–4 minIndividual

    Name the style

    Hand out page 1. Each student looks at the photo and the versions A, B and C, and writes five words for the style of each in the table: colours, lines, what is shown and what is left out. No talking yet.

  2. Step 24–8 minPairs

    Match the codes

    Pairs compare their words, then match A, B and C to three of the five codes on page 1. Two codes are not used.

  3. Step 38–11 minAll

    One word, a whole style

    Give the answers: A is /blueprint, B is /forceanalysis, C is /cutaway. Ask whose words came closest. Then explain that each version came from the same photo and one word. The image model turns the word into a style because it was trained on huge numbers of pictures that people drew, photographed and labelled. Then tell them that the original isn't a photo either: it was made with an image tool from a text prompt.

    Say You needed five words to describe the style. The tool needed one. Both of you got the style from work that people made.

  4. Step 411–19 minPairs

    Try a code

    Pairs photograph an object on their desk, such as a pencil case, a water bottle or a calculator: never a person. Before sending it, they agree on what they expect the code to do. Then they send the photo to the AI tool your school has approved with one code from page 1 and nothing else, and compare the result with what they expected.

    Prompts to try (pick one)

    • /cutaway
    • /blueprint
    • /xray

    Without AI: Show the Visual Codes page on the projector. For each of three cards, pairs say what the code will do to the photo before you move the slider, then compare.

  5. Step 519–28 minPairs

    Who made this?

    Pairs go through the six cases on page 2. For each, they tick who mostly made it, a person, both or AI, and tick the last column if they would say that AI was used in a school task. They agree on a reason for every case they first disagree about.

  6. Step 628–37 minAll

    What the rules say

    Take the cases one by one and ask for hands. Then go through the rules (see Background). In the US, copyright protects what a person made, not purely AI-generated material, and prompts alone are not enough; choosing, arranging and changing the output can count. A court in Beijing protected an image made with more than 150 prompts and later adjustments, and UK law protects some computer-generated works. Who owns AI-made work depends on the country. Saying that AI was used is a separate question: under the EU's AI Act, organisations and people who use AI to make deepfakes in their work must say that the content is AI-made (purely personal use is not covered), and schools set their own rules for schoolwork. Agree on a line the class can use, such as: Made with [tool] from my photo, code /cutaway.

    Say Who made it and who owns it are two different questions. Saying how you used AI is how you answer the first one honestly.

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 2 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which words described the styles best?

    Students often say

    • Lines and a grid for the blueprint.
    • The arrows in B.
    • In C you can see inside the plane.

  • Who made the picture of the object on your desk: you, the AI tool or the people whose pictures it was trained on?

    Students often say

    • Me. I took the photo.
    • The AI did most of it.
    • All of us, in a way.

  • Which case was hardest to place, and why?

    Students often say

    • The one I edited for an hour.
    • The sketch coloured in by AI.
    • The song, because it sounds like the singer.

  • When must you say that AI was used?

    Students often say

    • Always in schoolwork.
    • When it looks real.
    • When someone could be fooled.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Do steps 1 to 3 together on the projector. Skip the AI step and show three Visual Codes cards instead. Then sort the six cases as a class.

Harder

Pairs write a short AI disclosure for a school project that used AI in two places, and compare it with the school's own rules if there are any. Or they find out, from official sources, how their own country treats AI-made work.

Closing question

You made a picture with AI for a school poster. Who made it, and what would you write next to it?

Teaching in another language

Pages 1 and 2 have English text. The pictures need no translation. Write the column headings and the six cases on page 2 in your language next to the English ones, or read the cases aloud. The codes are English words: students type them as they are.

Background for you

Visual Codes are one-word prompts that Choosewise collected and tested with ChatGPT: you send a photo and a single word, such as /cutaway or /blueprint, and the image tool returns a new version of the same picture in that style. Other image tools may give different results, or none. The three versions on page 1 come from the Visual Codes page: A is /blueprint, B is /forceanalysis and C is /cutaway. Even the original is not a photo: it was made with an image tool from a text prompt. That makes a good question for the gather: who made the original?

ISTE's AI and Art project starts the same way: students describe an artist's style in their own words so that a friend could recognise it, then ask whether a computer could use the same description. An image model needs no such list: it was trained on huge numbers of pictures, made and labelled by people, and produces a style from a single word.

Who owns AI-assisted work differs between countries. The US Copyright Office (January 2025) concludes that copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, that prompts alone do not give enough control, and that people can protect what they add: their own work that shows in the output, and creative selection, arrangement or changes. The same report notes that the Beijing Internet Court in 2023 found an image made with Stable Diffusion protected, because of the more than 150 prompts and later adjustments behind it; that most EU member states see significant human input as necessary; and that UK law protects works generated by computer with no human author.

Saying that AI was used is a separate question. Under the EU's AI Act (Article 50), organisations and people who use AI to make deepfakes in their work, pictures, sound or video that look like real people, places or events, must disclose that the content was artificially generated or manipulated; the duty does not cover purely personal, non-professional use (Article 3); for clearly artistic, satirical or fictional work a lighter disclosure is enough. The European Commission says these transparency rules apply from August 2026 (checked 12 November 2026). Outside the EU, rules differ. For schoolwork, follow your school's rules; where there are none, a short line saying which tool was used, and for what, is a good habit.
