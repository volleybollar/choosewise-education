My Data Trail

Students map what apps record about them and what they get back, then work out what an app could guess from one invented week of data.

At a glance

Ages
13–15
Time
40 min
Groups
Individual, pairs, then the whole class
AI use
No AI
Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, our data trail and two closing-question tickets: one per pair
  • Page 2, Person X's week and our guesses: one per pair
  • Scrap paper, and the board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Tell the difference between data they share on purpose and data recorded in the background.
  • Explain what they get back for their data, and what an app can guess about someone from it.
  • Weigh what they get against what they give, and name one thing they want to change.

Key words

data trail
Everything apps and websites record about you, put together.
shared on purpose
Data you give an app knowingly, such as a post, a like or your birthday.
recorded in the background
Data an app records without you doing anything special, such as where your phone is, how long you watch or what you skip.
profile
What an app or a company puts together about you from your data, to predict what you will like or do.
terms and conditions
The rules you agree to when you start using an app, including what it may do with your data.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print pages 1 and 2 for every pair.
  • The sheets stay with the students and are not handed in; only the closing tickets are. Tell the class before they start.
  • Students write kinds of apps and kinds of data, never app names, usernames or their own details. Person X on page 2 is invented.
  • Draw a table on the board with the same four columns as page 1.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Yesterday's apps

    Each student writes down on scrap paper the kinds of apps they used yesterday, for example a video app, a game or a map.

    Say Write the kind of app, not its name, and never your username. Nobody will collect this.

  2. Step 23–6 minAll

    Two ways to collect

    Explain the two ways apps collect data, with a music app as the example on the board. Shared on purpose: the songs you like, your playlists. Recorded in the background: how long you listen, what time of day, what you skip. Then what you get back: suggestions, saved playlists.

  3. Step 36–16 minPairs

    Our data trail

    Hand out page 1. Pairs pick three of the app types in the table and fill in the three columns: kinds of data, not their own details.

  4. Step 416–22 minAll

    The class table

    Build a class table on the board from the pairs' answers, and add what they missed from the examples in the background, for example that a map app records where you live, your routine and when you arrive and leave.

  5. Step 522–32 minPairs

    What could an app guess?

    Hand out page 2. Person X is invented. Pairs read the eight things recorded in one week and write three guesses an app could make about Person X, which data each guess comes from, and whether they would be OK with an app making it.

  6. Step 632–37 minAll

    OK or not OK?

    Pairs share one guess they were OK with and one they were not. Point out that combined data says more than each piece, and that data can be passed on or sold to others. Ask who reads the terms and conditions of their apps, and why so few do.

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 1 and hands in the ticket only. The rest of page 1 stays with the students.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • What surprised you on the class table?

    Students often say

    • That apps know when I go to bed.
    • That the map knows where I live.
    • How much is recorded without me doing anything.

  • Which guess about Person X did not feel OK, and why?

    Students often say

    • The mood one: that's private.
    • Where they live.
    • That they're stressed, because then they get adverts for it.

  • Is it worth it?

    Students often say

    • For music suggestions, yes.
    • Not for a game that wants my location.
    • I didn't know I was paying with data.

  • What can you actually change?

    Students often say

    • Turn off location for apps that don't need it.
    • Not post my birthday and my pet's name.
    • Check what the app is allowed to use.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Fill in page 1 together on the board for one app type, then read Person X's week aloud and collect guesses as a class.

Harder

Pairs list three settings in one kind of app that they could change to share less, and what they would lose by changing them. Or they write the profile an advertiser might build of Person X, and the advert Person X would see next.

Closing question

Name one thing an app records about you in the background. What do you get back, and is it worth it?

Teaching in another language

Pages 1 and 2 have English text. Write the four column headings of page 1 in your class's language on the board, and say the app types aloud in it. Read Person X's week on page 2 aloud in your language, or write it on the board. Students can write their answers and their tickets in any language.

Background for you

Apps collect data in two ways. Some you share on purpose: a post, a like, a playlist, your birthday when you sign up. Some is recorded in the background: which profiles you look at, how long you watch, what time of day you use the app, where your phone is. ISTE's guide, which this lesson adapts, calls these footprints and fingerprints, terms from the book Blown to Bits by Hal Abelson, Ken Ledeen and Harry Lewis.

Apps with AI features use this data to predict what you will like and show it to you: the next video, songs, routes, adverts, friends to add. That is what you get back: convenience and suggestions that fit. The price is privacy. As ISTE's guide puts it, we don't always know if, how or why the data we share is used, or whether it is sold to someone else.

Combined data says more than each piece alone. ISTE's guide gives examples: posted photos and a list of favourite things can help someone guess passwords or the answers to security questions, and a company could draw conclusions about users' health from the topics they watch or read. Person X's week works the same way: no single line says much, but together they point to a sleep pattern, a hobby, a mood and a home address.

Examples for step 4, from ISTE's table. A social media app records likes, posts, follows and messages on purpose, and in the background the profiles and adverts you look at, the links you click, how long you stay, and when and where you use it. A video or music app records what you like, follow and save, and in the background how often you play something, the topics you prefer, and when and for how long you watch or listen. A map app records where you want to go, and in the background where you live, your routine and when you arrive and leave. A learning app records your answers and your progress, and in the background how long a topic takes you. A shopping app records what you buy and save for later, and in the background what you look at and for how long. A smartwatch or fitness app records your steps and heart rate, and in the background how they change between places, for example at school and at home.

What students can do: check which permissions an app has (location, microphone, contacts), switch off what it doesn't need, and think twice before posting details that answer security questions. Rules on children's data differ between countries.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

Nobody uses AI in this lesson. It is about the data that apps with AI features learn from and work with.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
