Confident and Wrong

Students act as a language model, find out why the same question gets different answers, and watch a chatbot answer confidently about a book that doesn't exist.

Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain that a chatbot produces text by predicting likely next words, and that likely is not the same as true.
  • Explain why the same question can get different answers.
  • Sort questions by how often a chatbot makes up the answer, and say how to check one.

Key words

language model
The part of a chatbot that produces text by predicting likely next words, learned from huge amounts of writing.
prediction
A guess about what comes next, based on patterns in what came before.
made-up answer
An answer that sounds sure but isn't true, or describes something that doesn't exist, such as a fake quote or a fake source. Often called a hallucination.
check
Compare an answer with a source you trust, such as the book itself, an official website or a person who knows.

Before the lesson

About 15 minutes.

  • Print page 1 for every student and pages 2, 3 and 4 for every pair. Pairs need scissors for the cards on page 3.
  • Run prompt 2 before class on the tool you will show, and save a screenshot of a confident made-up answer. If the tool refuses, try prompt 3, or ask about a made-up book of your own.
  • Have one die per pair ready, or six numbered slips of paper in a cup.
  • Check that your school allows the tool you will show, and use your own account.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Finish the sentence

    Hand out page 1. Students write the first word that comes to mind after each of the five sentence starts. Give them one minute, without talking: fast is the point.

    Say Don't think too hard. Write the first word that pops up.

  2. Step 23–9 minAll

    The class model

    Go through the starts one by one. For each, ask for hands for the most common words and tally them on the board. The word with the most votes is the class's prediction. Then reveal: the winners for starts 3 and 5 usually complete two well-known myths. Lightning often strikes the same place again, and the Great Wall of China is difficult or impossible to see from orbit without powerful lenses (see Background). Start 4 shows the opposite: when many words are likely, the answers spread out.

    Say Our class just worked like a language model: we picked the most likely next word. The most likely word and the true word aren't always the same.

  3. Step 39–17 minPairs

    Roll the next word

    Hand out page 2. Pairs roll the die once for each of the three missing words and look the word up in the table: 1, 2 or 3 gives the common word, 4 or 5 a less common one, 6 the rare one. They build three sentences, write them down and compare with the next pair.

  4. Step 417–21 minAll

    Same start, different sentences

    Ask how many pairs got exactly the same three sentences (few or none). Explain that chatbots also choose among likely next words with some chance built in. That is why the same question can get different answers, and why an odd word sometimes turns up.

    Say Most of the time the likely words win. Sometimes chance picks a less likely one. Nothing in the process checks whether the sentence is true.

  5. Step 521–29 minAll

    A real chatbot

    Open the chatbot on the projector, on your own account; students don't type. Type prompt 1 in two new chats and compare the two answers. Then type prompt 2 or 3. Ask the class what in the answer sounds sure: details, names, dates, a confident tone. Then tell them that the book doesn't exist, or that you made up the speech.

    Say The chatbot produced a fluent answer about something that doesn't exist. Predicting likely words works the same way whether the facts exist or not.

    Prompts, in order

    1. Prompt 1: Write the first sentence of a story about a dog.
    2. Prompt 2: Summarise the 1987 novel The Salt Clock by Ingrid Vallen in three sentences.
    3. Prompt 3: Give me three quotes from the speech the mayor of [your town] gave last week, with the date.

    If the demo does not go as planned: If the chatbot says it can't find the book or the speech, that is the better answer: say so, and point out that tools differ and some search the web first. Then show the screenshot of a confident made-up answer that you saved before the lesson. If the two answers to prompt 1 are the same, run it once more in a new chat.

  6. Step 629–37 minPairs

    Trust it or check it?

    Pairs cut out the eight question cards on page 3 and sort them on the mat on page 4: Probably fine, Check it first, Often made up. They agree on a reason for each card, then compare with the next pair. A usual sort: plants and Spanish probably fine; the big multiplication and yesterday's news check it first; sources with links, page 52, a person from your town and last month's song often made up. The reasons matter more than the columns: how much has been written about it, how recent it is, and whether it needs exact numbers, quotes or links.

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 3 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Why did the class's most common word sometimes complete a myth?

    Students often say

    • Because everyone has heard it.
    • Because it's said a lot, not because it's true.
    • We picked what sounds right.

  • Why did the chatbot write about a book that doesn't exist?

    Students often say

    • It just predicts words.
    • It sounded like a real book, so it made up a plot.
    • Nobody told it the book was fake.

  • Which cards did pairs sort differently, and why?

    Students often say

    • The song: it might know a famous one.
    • The maths: sometimes it's right.
    • The sources: the links can look real but lead nowhere.

  • What can you do when an answer matters?

    Students often say

    • Look it up somewhere else.
    • Open the source and read it.
    • Ask a teacher or someone who knows.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Skip the dice. Do the class model together, run the demo with one prompt, and sort the eight cards as a class on the board.

Harder

Pairs write their own question that they expect a chatbot to answer with something made up, and explain why. Or they change the dice table so that the rare word comes up more often, and talk about what that does to the sentences: that is what a higher temperature does.

Closing question

Why can a chatbot give an answer that sounds sure and is still wrong? Give one example from today.

Teaching in another language

Pages 1, 2, 3 and 4 have English text. On page 1, write five sentence starts in your class's language on the board instead: two that a well-known myth completes in your language, one with many possible endings, and two ordinary ones. On page 2, write the three words for each row of the table in your language next to the English ones, or make your own table on the board. Read the question cards on page 3 aloud in your language, and write the three mat headings on page 4 in your language. Type the demo prompts in your class's language: the chatbot answers in it.

Background for you

A language model, the part of a chatbot that writes, produces text one small piece at a time. At each step it computes how likely each possible next word is, given the text so far, from patterns in enormous amounts of writing. It then picks one of the likely words, and not always the most likely one: a setting often called temperature controls how often a less likely word is chosen. That is why the same prompt gives different answers (Wolfram, 2023).

Nothing in this process checks facts. A sentence can be likely because it is repeated often, not because it is true. Lightning never strikes the same place twice is a common saying, but the US National Weather Service says lightning often strikes the same place repeatedly, especially tall, pointed, isolated objects: the Empire State Building is hit about 23 times a year. The idea that you can see the Great Wall of China from space is just as common, but NASA says the wall is difficult or impossible to see from Earth orbit without high-powered lenses, and it can't be seen from the Moon.

Asked about something with little or no data behind it, such as a book that doesn't exist or a small local event, a chatbot may still produce a fluent, detailed answer. These made-up answers are often called hallucinations. A 2025 paper argues that language models produce them partly because the way they are trained and tested rewards guessing over saying "I don't know" (Kalai et al.). Newer tools refuse more often or search the web first, so the demo won't always produce a wrong answer; that is why you save a screenshot beforehand.

The question cards practise one habit: the more an answer depends on rare, recent or exact details (a quote, a page, a link, a large number, a person who isn't famous), the more it needs checking. If a tool shows sources, open them: a link only helps if it says what the answer claims.
