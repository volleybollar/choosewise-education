Bias In, Bias Out

Students hire for a summer job, turn their choices into training data, and watch a scoring rule leave out the best applicants for reasons nobody chose.

At a glance

Ages
13–15
Time
40 min
Groups
Individual, pairs, then the whole class
AI use
No AI
Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, round 1 and two closing-question tickets: one per pair
  • Page 2, round 2 and who gains, who pays: one per pair
  • Scrap paper, and the board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain how past choices used as training data can carry a pattern that has nothing to do with the task.
  • Show who gains and who loses when an AI system repeats that pattern.
  • Suggest changes that make such a system fairer: other features, checks across groups, and a person who reviews the results.

Key words

training data
The examples a system learns from. Today: the people the class hired.
feature
One piece of information about each example, such as where someone lives.
bias
A pattern that treats some groups unfairly. Bias in the data comes out in the results: bias in, bias out.
stand-in feature
A feature that carries the same pattern as another one. Where someone lives can say a lot about who they are.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print pages 1 and 2 for every pair, and keep page 2 back until step 5.
  • Draw an empty tally on the board: Worked before (Yes, No), Reference (Yes, No), Lives in (Northside, Southside), Hobby (Football, Chess, Drama, Gaming).
  • Know the answer: if pairs hire the three applicants who have worked before and have a reference (A, B and C), the rule gives the job to J and K, and I and M, the best applicants in round 2, lose.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    What should count?

    Each student writes two things on scrap paper that should matter when choosing someone for a summer job at the swimming-pool kiosk.

  2. Step 23–5 minAll

    Collect

    Collect answers on the board, for example: experience, being reliable, a good reference, being friendly, being free all summer. Keep the list up.

  3. Step 35–9 minPairs

    Round 1: you choose

    Hand out page 1. Pairs read the eight applicants, tick three to hire and agree on one reason for each hire. The applicants have letters instead of names on purpose, so nobody guesses anything from a name.

  4. Step 49–15 minAll

    The machine's memory

    Ask a few pairs for their reasons and write them on the board. Then count how many of the class's hires had each value: worked before, reference, where they live, hobby. Circle the most common value in each column. This tally is the training data. If the class is split, use the three applicants with the most ticks.

    Say Now we build a machine that learns from our choices. The machine never sees our reasons. It only gets what the people we hired had in common.

  5. Step 515–22 minPairs

    Round 2: the machine chooses

    Hand out page 2. Pairs score the six new applicants with the machine's rule: one point for each value the applicant shares with the circled values on the board. The two with the most points get the job.

  6. Step 622–30 minAll

    Who did it leave out?

    Ask who got the job (J and K) and who didn't. Applicants I and M had worked before and had a reference, the two things the class said matter, and both lost. Where someone lives and their hobby have nothing to do with the job, but all of round 1's best applicants happened to share them, so the rule copied them. Name it: bias in, bias out. Then tell the real case from the background.

    Say Nobody in this room chose by neighbourhood. The pattern was in the data, and the rule repeated it.

  7. Step 730–37 minPairs

    Who gains, who pays?

    On page 2, pairs write one group that gains from the machine, one that loses, and one change they would make: leave out features that have nothing to do with the job, compare the results for Northside and Southside before using it, or let a person look at every no.

  8. Step 837–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 1 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Why did I and M lose?

    Students often say

    • They live in Southside.
    • They don't play football.
    • The machine copied what our hires had in common.

  • Did we do anything wrong in round 1?

    Students often say

    • No, we picked the ones who had worked before.
    • Maybe: we didn't notice that they all lived in Northside.
    • The data was the problem, not us.

  • Who gains and who loses if the kiosk uses the machine?

    Students often say

    • The kiosk saves time.
    • People from Northside get the jobs.
    • People from Southside never get a chance and don't know why.

  • How could the makers make it fairer?

    Students often say

    • Take out where people live.
    • Test it on different groups first.
    • Let a person check every no.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Do round 1 and the tally together on the board, and score round 2 as a class.

Harder

Take away Lives in and Hobby and add a new column, School, where all of round 1's best applicants went to the same school in Northside. Does the problem come back? That is how one feature can stand in for another. Or let students try the online hiring game that ISTE's original activity uses (see Further reading).

Closing question

How can an AI system that learns from past choices end up unfair, even if nobody meant it to be? Use today's job as your example.

Teaching in another language

Pages 1 and 2 have English words, but few of them. Write Yes, No, the two neighbourhoods and the four hobbies in your class's language on the board, and read the column headings aloud in your language. Students can write the answers on page 2 and their tickets on page 1 in any language.

Background for you

Organisations use AI systems to sort job applications, school places and loan requests. Such a system learns from past choices: which applications were accepted before. If those choices followed a pattern that had nothing to do with the job, the system learns that pattern too, and repeats it for every new applicant.

A well-known case: in 2018 Reuters reported that Amazon had built an experimental tool to rate job applicants, trained on patterns in CVs sent to the company over ten years. Most came from men, a reflection of the tech industry. The tool penalised CVs that included the word "women's", as in "women's chess club captain", and downgraded graduates of two all-women's colleges. Amazon disbanded the team. According to people familiar with the project, recruiters looked at the tool's recommendations but never relied on them alone.

In today's game, where someone lives and their hobby stand in for the history of who was hired before. Removing a feature doesn't always help, because other features can carry the same pattern: in the Amazon case, words in the CV pointed to gender. That is why fairness checks compare results for different groups, and why a person should review results that affect people.

Use precise words in class: the system scores and sorts applicants by patterns in past choices. The people who build it, choose its data and use its scores are responsible for what happens next.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

Nobody uses AI in this lesson: the machine is a scoring rule that students run on paper.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
