Using AI Well

Students write a short email themselves first, get two versions from AI, and go through the AI text sentence by sentence: accept, revise or reject.

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, my email and my final email: one per student

  • One device per pair with the AI tool your school has approved
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Do a task themselves before asking AI, so that they have something to compare the AI's answer with.
  • Get a better answer by giving AI the context it needs, for example by asking it to ask questions first.
  • Accept, revise or reject AI text sentence by sentence, and give a reason for each choice.

Key words

context
What an AI tool can't know unless you tell it: who you are, who the text is for and what really happened.
made up
A detail in the AI text that you never gave it, such as a reason, a name or a date.
accept, revise or reject
Keep a sentence as it is, change it, or leave it out.
clarifying question
A question that asks for missing information before you start. You can ask an AI tool to ask you its questions first.

Before the lesson

About 15 minutes.

  • Print page 1 for every student and page 2 for every pair.
  • Before class, run both prompts in step 2 on the tool students will use, prompt 2 in a new chat, and answer its questions with an invented situation. Print the two answers or have them ready on the projector. You need them if the tool is blocked or students can't log in.
  • Check that your school has approved the tool for students' age and that they can log in.
  • Tell students before step 1 that the reason in their email can be real or made up, and that they don't put names, health details or anything private into the AI tool. Page 1 says so too.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–6 minIndividual

    Do it yourself first

    Hand out page 1. Each student writes an email to a teacher asking for three more days on an assignment, one sentence per row: a greeting, the request, a reason, an offer and a sign-off. The reason can be real or made up for the lesson.

    Say Nothing private goes into the AI tool later, so pick a reason you're happy to share, or make one up.

  2. Step 26–16 minPairs

    Two prompts

    Pairs open the AI tool your school has approved. They type prompt 1 from page 2 in a new chat and read the email. Then they open another new chat, type prompt 2 and answer the questions it asks, without names or private details. They compare the two AI emails with each other and with their own.

    Prompts, in order

    1. Prompt 1: Write an email to my teacher asking for three more days on my assignment.
    2. Prompt 2: I need to email my teacher to ask for three more days on an assignment. Before you write anything, ask me the questions you need answered. Then write the email.

    Without AI: Hand out or show the two answers you saved before class. Pairs read the email from prompt 1, then the questions and the email from prompt 2, and compare them with their own emails.

  3. Step 316–25 minPairs

    Accept, revise or reject

    Pairs take the better AI email and go through it sentence by sentence on page 2: accept, revise or reject, and why. In the Why? column they also note anything the AI text made up, such as a reason, a date or a promise that nobody gave it.

  4. Step 425–29 minIndividual

    My final email

    Each student writes the final email on page 1. They can keep their own sentences, take sentences from the AI text or change them; what matters is that they can explain each choice.

  5. Step 529–37 minAll

    Who answers for it?

    Ask pairs where the AI text added something and where it didn't. Usual answers: a clear structure and a polite tone; prompt 1's email made up a reason or sounded too formal; prompt 2's questions made its email fit the situation better. Then ask what doing it themselves first gave them: something to compare with, and their own voice.

    Say You send the email, so you answer for every sentence in it, including the ones the AI wrote.

  6. Step 637–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 2 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which email was best: yours, prompt 1's or prompt 2's? Why?

    Students often say

    • Prompt 2's, because we told it the situation.
    • Mine. It sounded like me.
    • Prompt 1's was too long and too formal.

  • What did the AI text make up?

    Students often say

    • A family emergency.
    • That I'd been ill.
    • A date we never gave it.

  • What did prompt 2 do differently?

    Students often say

    • It asked what had happened first.
    • It asked who the teacher is.
    • It asked how formal it should be.

  • When is it worth doing a task yourself first?

    Students often say

    • When I want it to sound like me.
    • When it's what I'm meant to learn.
    • When the AI doesn't know the situation.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Run the two prompts as a teacher demo on the projector and give each pair a printout of the second email. Pairs mark accept, revise or reject on the printout.

Harder

Pairs use the same method on a task from one of their courses, such as the introduction to a lab report or a summary of a chapter. Before they start, they choose which part they will do themselves and which part AI may help with, and afterwards they explain whether that split worked.

Closing question

When is it worth doing a task yourself before you ask AI? Use today's email as your example.

Teaching in another language

Pages 1 and 2 have English text. Students can write the email in your class's language and type the prompts in that language: the AI tool answers in it. Write the column headings on page 2 in your language next to the English ones.

Background for you

The OECD and European Commission's AI literacy framework (AILit) names three things to do with any AI output: accept it, revise it or reject it. At the advanced level, students justify each choice. The framework's classroom examples also have students work on their own before they look at AI-generated ideas, because what they bring themselves is what they compare the AI's answer with.

A chatbot writes from what is in the chat and, in some tools, from what it has saved from earlier chats. Asked for an email asking for more time, it fills the gaps in the prompt: if no reason is given, the email may include one anyway, such as an illness or a family matter, because emails like this usually have one. Prompt 2 asks the tool to ask its questions first, so the student fills the gaps instead. Tools differ, and the same prompt gives different answers each time, so expect pairs' emails to differ.

Keep the privacy rule simple: nothing goes into an AI tool that you wouldn't write on the board. That means no names of teachers or classmates, no health details and nothing about family or friends. In the email, [Teacher] and [Name] can stay as placeholders. Some tools also save details from earlier chats, which is one more reason to keep private things out.

Framework links

AILit: Engage with AI, competence 3 (evaluate whether AI outputs should be accepted, revised or rejected, justifying each choice, advanced level); Create with AI, competence 3 (direct generative AI systems to refine results, comparing outputs with their own ideas, intermediate level); Manage AI, competence 4 (monitor and evaluate AI use, comparing AI outputs with the result they want, intermediate level)
