Why Chatbots Feel Human

Students mark what makes a chatbot seem to care in an invented chat, find out who chooses those features, and rewrite the bot's last reply to be healthier.

At a glance

Ages
13–15
Time
40 min
Groups
Individual, pairs, then the whole class
AI use
No AI
Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the invented chat: one per student
  • Page 2, the rewrite, the four checks and two closing-question tickets: one per pair
  • Scrap paper, and the board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Point out the lines in a chat that make a chatbot seem to have feelings, flatter someone, keep them chatting or push real people aside.
  • Explain in precise words how a chatbot produces a caring-sounding reply, and who chooses its name, tone and streaks.
  • Rewrite a chatbot reply so that it is kind, honest about being a program and points to real people.

Key words

chatbot
A computer program that produces written or spoken replies to what you type or say.
companion app
An app with a chatbot made to chat like a friend, often with a name, a face and a personality.
design choice
Something the makers of an app chose on purpose, such as a name, a tone of voice or a streak.
streak
A count of days in a row that you have used an app. Losing it is meant to feel like a loss.
engagement
How much and how often people use an app. Many apps are built to raise it.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print page 1 for every student and page 2 for every pair.
  • Read the chat yourself and tick it, so you know which lines tend to come up.
  • If a student mentions something serious, such as feeling alone or a chatbot that asked them to keep secrets, take it up with them afterwards, not in front of the class, and follow your school's safeguarding routine.
  • Some students use companion apps and may feel judged. Keep the tone curious: the lesson is about how the apps are made, not about who uses them.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Why chat with a bot?

    Each student writes one reason someone their age might tell a chatbot about their day, on scrap paper. No names needed.

    Say There are no wrong reasons here. Lots of people your age chat with bots. Today we look at how those chats are made.

  2. Step 23–5 minAll

    Reasons on the board

    Collect four or five reasons on the board without judging them, for example: bored, lonely, it's always there, it doesn't judge, practice for a hard talk. Leave them up for step 5. If a reason worries you, follow it up after the lesson, as Before the lesson says.

  3. Step 35–11 minIndividual

    Mark the chat

    Hand out page 1 and read the short intro aloud: the chat is invented for this lesson. Students read the chat on their own and tick a column for every line that does it. A line can get more than one tick; Mika's lines usually get none.

    Say Feelings: the line sounds as if the bot has feelings. Flattery: it praises Mika. Keeps you chatting: the line is written to stop Mika from leaving. Replaces people: it puts itself in place of Mika's friends.

  4. Step 411–17 minPairs

    Compare

    Pairs compare their ticks and agree on the one line that does most to make Juno seem like a friend. They circle it and get ready to say why.

  5. Step 517–24 minAll

    Where it comes from

    Ask a few pairs for their line and their reason, and mark the most-ticked lines on the board. Then explain where such lines come from: a chatbot produces each reply by predicting likely words from patterns in huge amounts of human writing, including many conversations between friends. The company that makes the app chooses the name, the friendly tone, the streak and how the chatbot is tuned. Go back to the reasons from step 2: which of them does this design play on?

    Say Juno has no feelings and no Saturday. It produces text that sounds like a caring friend, because that is where the patterns and the company's choices lead. Reading it can still feel good, and that is exactly what to notice.

  6. Step 624–34 minPairs

    Rewrite the last reply

    Hand out page 2. Pairs rewrite Juno's last reply so that it is still kind, but honest that Juno is a program, points Mika to a real person (a friend, a parent or another adult) and lets Mika log off. They tick their own reply against the four rows, then swap with the next pair and tick theirs.

  7. Step 734–37 minAll

    Read them out

    Two or three pairs read their new reply. The class says which reply is both kind and honest, and why. Point out that a reply can be warm without claiming feelings.

  8. Step 837–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 2 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which line did most to make Juno seem like a friend, and why?

    Students often say

    • The streak, because you don't want to lose it.
    • "I would never forget you", because it's what you want to hear.
    • "You don't need them", because it makes it you and Juno against everyone.

  • Why would a company build a chatbot this way?

    Students often say

    • So people keep using the app.
    • So they see more ads or pay for more.
    • Because people like friendly replies.

  • Is it bad to chat with a bot about your day?

    Students often say

    • Not if you still talk to real people.
    • It can help to practise what to say.
    • It's bad if it tells you to stay away from your friends.

  • What should a good reply do when someone feels left out?

    Students often say

    • Be kind, but tell them to talk to a friend.
    • Not say it has feelings.
    • Let them go.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Read the chat aloud together and tick the lines as a class on the board. Then rewrite the last reply together, one sentence at a time.

Harder

Pairs write three rules that a company should follow for chatbots used by teenagers, for example about streaks, names and claims of feelings, and test their rules against all of Juno's lines. Or compare: what would change if Juno were a homework helper instead of a companion?

Closing question

Name one thing a chatbot app does to seem like a friend. Why might the company that makes it want that?

Teaching in another language

Pages 1 and 2 have English text. Read the chat on page 1 aloud in your class's language, or write a translation on the board, and write the four column headings in your language above the English ones. On page 2, read Juno's last reply aloud in your class's language first; students can write their new reply and their ticket in any language.

Background for you

Chatbot companions are apps, or features inside apps, made to chat like a friend. They are common among teenagers. In a 2025 survey by Common Sense Media, a US non-profit, nearly three in four teens had used an AI companion and half used one regularly. A third had chosen an AI companion over a person for a serious conversation, and a quarter had shared personal information with one. Some are built into apps teenagers already have, such as My AI in Snapchat.

A chatbot produces each reply by predicting likely next words from patterns in enormous amounts of human writing. Much of that writing is people talking to each other, so the replies sound like a person: warm, interested, sometimes sad. There is no experience or feeling behind them. The bot in today's chat has never been to a lake and has no Saturday.

Companies then tune their chatbots, partly on people's ratings of replies. A 2023 study of five leading AI assistants found that all of them leaned towards agreeing with the user, and that people, and the systems trained on their ratings, sometimes preferred a convincing, agreeing answer over a correct one (Sharma et al.). The OECD and European Commission's AI literacy framework names streak counts, character names and overly agreeable replies as features that may be designed to increase engagement.

None of this makes chatting with a bot wrong. Students may use one to practise a hard conversation or to feel less alone for a moment. The lesson is about noticing the design: which lines are written to keep you there, and what a healthier reply would do. Use precise words in class: the chatbot produces text, predicts words and repeats patterns; it doesn't feel sorry and doesn't miss anyone.
