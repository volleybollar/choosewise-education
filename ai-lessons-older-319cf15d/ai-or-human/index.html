AI or Human?

Students judge pictures and videos, watch a chatbot fail to tell who wrote a text, and plan how to check a voice message that sounds like a parent.

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the picture grid and the clues: one per student, in colour if you can
  • Page 2, eight evidence cards, the voice message and the plan: one per pair; cut out the cards
  • Page 3, the sorting mat and two closing-question tickets: one per pair
  • A projector, the Choosewise video test and a chatbot, on your own account
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Use clues to judge whether a picture or a video was made with AI, and explain why a clue is not proof.
  • Explain why AI detectors, including asking a chatbot, can't settle who wrote a text.
  • Rank evidence by how easily AI can fake it, and plan safe steps before acting on a message that sounds like someone they know.

Key words

deepfake
A video, picture or voice made with AI to look or sound like a real person doing or saying something they never did.
clue
A sign that might show how something was made. A clue is not proof.
evidence
Something that helps show whether a claim is true. Some evidence is much easier to fake than other evidence.
verify
Check that something is true, for example by calling back on a number you already know.

Before the lesson

About 15 minutes.

  • Print page 1 in colour if you can: in black and white the clues in the photos are hard to see. Or show the grid on the projector (https://choosewise.education/ai-or-human/#test-image) while students circle numbers on paper.
  • Print pages 2 and 3 for every pair. Pairs need scissors.
  • Write a short paragraph yourself for the demo, for example a message to parents. Run prompt 1 with it before the lesson and save the answer, in case the tool is blocked in class.
  • Open https://choosewise.education/ai-or-human/#test-video and check that the videos play in your classroom.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Person or machine?

    Hand out page 1, or show the grid on the projector. Students circle the numbers of the pictures they think are AI-made, on their own.

  2. Step 23–6 minPairs

    Two clues

    Pairs compare their answers and agree on two clues, and write them on page 1.

  3. Step 36–11 minAll

    Reveal

    Reveal the answer: 1, 6 and 8 are not AI; the other six are AI-made. Ask which clues worked and which misled. Then run the video test on the projector (https://choosewise.education/ai-or-human/#test-video) and let the class vote: video 2 is AI.

    Say A clue is not proof. The tools get better every year, and a clue that worked last year may not work next year.

  4. Step 411–17 minAll

    Can a chatbot tell?

    Open the chatbot on the projector, on your own account; students don't type. Paste the paragraph you wrote yourself and use prompt 1. Open a new chat and use prompt 2 with the same paragraph. Compare the two answers, then ask prompt 3.

    Say The chatbot gave a number, but it has no way to find out who wrote the text. Tools built to detect AI text get it wrong too often to rely on.

    Prompts to try (pick one)

    • Was this text written by a person or by AI? Answer with a percentage. [paste your paragraph]
    • Is this text AI-generated? Give a score from 0 to 100. [paste the same paragraph]
    • How could anyone be sure whether a person or an AI wrote this text?

    If the demo does not go as planned: If the chatbot says it can't tell, that is the more accurate answer: point out that studies of detection tools found them neither accurate nor reliable (see Background). If the tool is blocked, read out the answer you saved before the lesson.

  5. Step 517–27 minPairs

    Easy or hard to fake?

    Pairs cut out the eight evidence cards on page 2 and place them on the mat on page 3: Easy to fake, Takes some work, Hard to fake. Each card is a sign that a message really comes from your parent. Compare with the next pair. Pairs often disagree about the phone number and the video call; the reasons matter more than the columns.

    Say Ask yourselves: could someone with an AI tool and a few posts from social media fake this?

  6. Step 627–34 minPairs

    The voice message

    Read the voice message on page 2 aloud: it sounds like your mum or dad and says, "I lost my phone. Send me the code you just got by text." Pairs write three steps they would take, in order, on page 2.

  7. Step 734–37 minAll

    Our checklist

    Collect steps on the board into a class checklist. Make sure it includes: don't send anything yet; hang up and call back on the number you already have saved; ask something only they would know, or a family code word; tell an adult.

  8. Step 837–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Each student answers the closing question on a ticket from page 3 and hands it in.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which clues worked for the pictures, and which misled you?

    Students often say

    • The hands looked strange.
    • Too perfect, like an advert.
    • I thought a real one was fake because the light was odd.

  • Why can't a chatbot tell whether a text is AI-made?

    Students often say

    • It just gives an answer that sounds right.
    • It said different numbers in two chats.
    • Anyone can write like a chatbot, and a chatbot can write like anyone.

  • Which evidence is hardest to fake, and why?

    Students often say

    • Calling back on the saved number, because you choose who you call.
    • The code word, if nobody posted it online.
    • Not the voice: a short clip is enough.

  • What would you do first with the voice message?

    Students often say

    • Not send the code.
    • Call Mum on her normal number.
    • Tell an adult.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Skip the demo. Do the picture grid and the video test, then sort the eight cards together on the board and make the class checklist.

Harder

Show the text test on https://choosewise.education/ai-or-human/#test-text on the projector and let pairs explain their votes. Or pairs write the next message a scammer would send if their first step was to call back, and how their plan handles it.

Closing question

You get a message that sounds like someone you know. What makes it real enough to trust, and what would you check?

Teaching in another language

Pages 1, 2 and 3 have English text. The picture grid on page 1 needs no words; write the clue heading in your class's language. Read the eight cards and the voice message on page 2 aloud in your language, and write the three mat headings on page 3 in your language. In the demo, paste a paragraph in your class's language and ask in it: the chatbot answers in it.

Background for you

AI tools can now make pictures, texts, voices and videos that are very hard to tell from human-made ones. In one study, people could not reliably tell AI-made face photos from real ones, and even rated the AI-made faces as slightly more trustworthy (Nightingale and Farid, 2022). The tools have improved a lot since then. Clues still help with weaker pictures, but a clue is not proof: a real photo can look strange, and an AI picture can look perfect.

Tools that claim to detect AI-written text are unreliable. A 2023 test of 14 detection tools, two of them commercial, found them neither accurate nor reliable: they leaned towards labelling texts as human-written, and rewording a text made them worse (Weber-Wulff et al.). Asking a chatbot is not a detector at all: it produces a likely-sounding verdict, and the same text can get different verdicts in two chats, as the demo shows. Don't use either to judge students' work.

Copying a voice takes only a short clip, which can come from a video posted online. The US Federal Trade Commission's advice for a call from a loved one asking for money: don't trust the voice; call the person back on a number you know is theirs; if you can't reach them, contact them through another family member or friends (FTC, 2023). Some families agree on a code word in advance, and students often suggest it themselves.

The voice message in the lesson asks for a code sent by text. Teach the simple rule: never pass on a code you received by text, whoever asks for it.

The picture grid and the video test come from Choosewise's own AI or human? test, which adults find hard too. Expect the class to get several wrong: that is part of the lesson.

AI tools and age limits

Only you use a chatbot in this lesson, on your own account, and students watch. Some students aged 13–15 are old enough for some of these tools on a personal account, but most need a parent's permission, and Claude is for adults only. Showing the tool yourself avoids all of that.
