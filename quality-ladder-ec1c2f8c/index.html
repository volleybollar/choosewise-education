Quality Ladder

Describe quality once, for the whole class. Your AI tool writes three made-up answers to your task at three levels. Students write their own first, rank the three against your criteria, and lift their own answer one level. No grading, and no student work in the AI.

What a ladder looks like

History · Grade 8

The task
Why did the Industrial Revolution begin in Britain? Explain with at least two reasons.
Criteria
  1. Gives reasons
  2. Links each reason to an effect
  3. Uses evidence

What students get: three samples

Sample A

One reason is that Britain had a lot of coal. Coal could power steam engines, so factories no longer had to be built next to fast rivers to drive their machines. Another reason is that Britain had colonies. The colonies were very important for trade. Together these two things meant that Britain was ready to change how goods were made before other countries.

Sample B

Britain had lots of coal and iron. It also had factories, steam engines and canals. There were many inventors, like James Watt. Britain had colonies all over the world and a big navy, and it had banks too. All of these things are why the Industrial Revolution started in Britain and not in other countries, because Britain had more of them than anywhere else.

Sample C

Britain had three advantages. First, it had large coalfields, and coal powered steam engines: Newcomen's engine (1712) was first used to pump water out of coal mines. Second, trade with its colonies brought in raw cotton, so cotton makers wanted faster spinning machines, like Arkwright's water frame (1769). Third, a growing population gave factories both workers and customers. Together, these made Britain the first to industrialise.

Teacher key · not for students

  • Sample A Middle

    Gives two reasons and links coal to an effect. The colonies are only named, and there is no fact or date as evidence.

    One level up: Say what the colonies did for industry, and back each link with a fact from the chapter.

  • Sample B Weakest

    Lists many causes but links none of them to an effect, so it never explains why. This is the common mistake.

    One level up: Pick two causes and say what each one made possible.

  • Sample C Strongest

    Three reasons, each linked to an effect, two backed by a fact and a date, and a short conclusion.

    One level up: Back the third reason with a fact too, and say which reason mattered most, and why.

How it works

  1. Enter the task and your criteria. Copy the prompt into your AI tool, and read the three samples before the lesson: check the facts and that the levels differ in what they do, not in length.
  2. In class, students write their own answer first, then rank Samples A, B and C and explain the differences with the criteria. Then you reveal the levels.
  3. Students lift their own answer one level. You give whole-class feedback instead of thirty separate comments.

The lesson, step by step

  1. Write your own answer to the task first, before you see any samples. Use books and notes if the task allows.
  2. Read Samples A, B and C. On your own, rank them from strongest to weakest.
  3. With a partner, explain your ranking using the criteria: what does the strongest one do that the others do not?
  4. Your teacher reveals the levels, shows a second strong answer that does the task in a different way (when there is one), and leads a short discussion with three questions.
  5. Decide which sample your own answer is closest to, and why.
  6. Make the one change that would lift your answer one level, and write your new version.

Grades 1–3: read the samples aloud and rank them together. Students say or draw what makes the best one better, then improve their own answer with your help.

About 25 minutes of one lesson (our estimate).

Whole-class feedback

Framework

Read the answers once, make notes on this template as you go, and give the class one round of feedback instead of thirty separate comments.

Task and date
Which task the feedback is about, and which Quality Ladder samples it refers to.
What many did well
Two or three things to keep doing, each with a short example in your own words.
Misunderstandings to reteach
Ideas many students got wrong. These need teaching again, not a correction in the margin.
Careless mistakes
Slips students can fix themselves once they know where to look. Keep them apart from misunderstandings.
One thing everyone does next
A single task every student does in the next lesson, with time set aside to do it.
Students to talk to
Who needs a word with you. Write names on paper or in this document, never in an AI tool.

Start the next lesson with the feedback, then give students time to act on it: redo a part, fix the misunderstanding or lift their answer one level.

A one-page feedback policy

Framework

Feedback policy for [School], agreed by [Team] on [Date]. Change it to fit your school, and keep it to one page.

  1. Feedback is for the student's next step. Feedback exists to move learning forward. The amount of feedback is not the same as its quality. DfE 2016
  2. Teachers choose the method and how often. Written, verbal and whole-class feedback all count. The teacher decides what fits the task; this policy sets no frequency. EEF 2021
  3. No marking for show. No acknowledgement marking and no deep-marking trails in different colour pens. DfE 2016; Elliott et al. 2016
  4. Students act on feedback. Feedback is followed by time in a lesson to use it. Elliott et al. 2016
  5. Not a grade on every piece. Not every piece of work gets a grade, so that students read the comments. Elliott et al. 2016
  6. Students judge quality too. Students regularly compare work with the criteria, both their own and samples, before the teacher gives feedback. Sanchez et al. 2017
  7. AI drafts, it does not judge. AI may draft samples, templates and wording. It never sees student work or names, and it never grades. Choosewise
  8. Review. [Team] reviews this policy on [Review date].

Use the same samples with colleagues

Bring one set of three samples to a department meeting. Each teacher places a few real answers next to them (weaker, similar or stronger), then you compare where you put them and why. In a writing study, rating texts against benchmark texts at different levels gave scores that agreed closely with comparative judgement, another careful method, although ratings bunched in the middle (Bouwer et al. 2023). One meeting can give the team a shared picture of what a strong answer looks like.

What the research says

Students judging work against criteria is well supported in school. Samples written by AI at three levels have not been tested yet.

Quality Ladder as a whole: Promising, with a Strong core.

  • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that working this way improves learning.
  • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
  • Framework A useful structure. The method itself has not been tested in intervention studies.
  • Choosewise Our own rule, not a research finding.

Strong Judging work against criteria helps students learn. Students in Grades 3–12 who assessed their own or each other's work did better on later tests, self-assessment slightly more than peer assessment (0.34 and 0.29; Sanchez et al. 2017). Across 54 control-group studies from primary school to university, peer assessment helped performance (0.31 overall, and 0.28 compared with teacher assessment alone; Double et al. 2020). In writing in Grades 1–8, feedback from adults helped most (0.87), then feedback from students themselves (0.62) and from peers (0.58), while teachers only monitoring progress did not help (Graham et al. 2015). It works best with criteria and some training. Sources: Sanchez et al. 2017, Journal of Educational Psychology, Double, McGrane & Hopfenbeck 2020, Educational Psychology Review, Graham, Hebert & Harris 2015, The Elementary School Journal

Strong Clear criteria help, when they are about substance. Rubrics improved academic performance across 21 studies in school and university (0.45 after correcting for publication bias), more than they improved self-regulation (0.23) or self-efficacy (0.18) (Panadero et al. 2023). In one Year 6 class, success criteria turned into a checklist that students ticked off (Eltringham et al. 2018). Criteria should describe what good thinking does, not features to count. Sources: Panadero et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review, Eltringham, Hawe & Dixon 2018, Education 3-13

Promising Samples work best after a first draft and a discussion. A review of 40 studies of exemplars recommends that students write a draft first, then compare it with the samples and improve it. All 40 studies were in higher education, and most had no control group (To et al. 2022). Among university students, a teacher-led discussion of why each sample sits at its level raised understanding and results, while just handing out grade descriptors may have little or no benefit (Hendry et al. 2012). Sources: To, Panadero & Carless 2022, Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, Hendry, Armstrong & Bromberger 2012, Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education

Promising AI-written model texts: one promising study, no test of three levels. In a study with 104 high-school English learners, a ChatGPT-written model text improved writing as much as a teacher-written one, though students worried that its style was unnatural (Lu & Zeng 2025). No study of AI-written samples at three levels was found. That is why you read every sample before the lesson. Source: Lu & Zeng 2025, Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching

Framework Whole-class feedback saves time. Its effect on learning has not been tested in a controlled study. In one English secondary school, reading all books and giving whole-class feedback cut a maths teacher's marking from about 3.5 to 1 hour a week. That is a worked estimate without attainment data (Jones & Essery 2018). The EEF judges that how feedback is delivered is likely to matter less than its principles, and warns that whole-class feedback can become too generic (EEF 2021). No controlled study of its effect on learning was found. Sources: Jones & Essery 2018, Chartered College of Teaching, Education Endowment Foundation 2021, Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning

Promising The same samples help colleagues agree. Rating texts against benchmark texts at different levels gave scores that agreed closely with comparative judgement, although ratings bunched in the middle (Bouwer et al. 2023). Source: Bouwer et al. 2023, Journal of Writing Research

Watch out for

  • Students copy the strongest sample. Students write before they see any sample, a second strong answer shows another way to do it well, and each new task gets a new set.
  • The criteria become a checklist. Write criteria about what good thinking does, and ask students to explain why a sample sits where it does, not to tick off features.
  • Samples narrow ideas in open, creative tasks. Use the ladder for tasks where students explain, argue or solve. For very open, creative tasks, use only the discussion questions (To et al. 2022).
  • The AI gets facts wrong or misjudges the age. Read all three samples before the lesson. Check the facts, the language for the age and the fit to your task, and cut or rewrite what does not fit (in one study, students found AI model texts unnatural in style; Lu & Zeng 2025).

AI tools and age rules

You use AI to prepare. Students need no AI for Quality Ladder. If students use AI tools, these rules apply (November 2026):

  • Copilot Chat: Students aged 13 and over, through the school's own Microsoft 365 Education account. The school's administrator must set each student's age group; under-13 accounts get no access.
  • Gemini for Education: Open to education users of all ages, with stricter content rules and some features switched off for under-18s. The school's administrator must turn it on for students under 18.
  • ChatGPT: Ages 13 and over (or the minimum age in your country) on personal accounts, with a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. Users under 18 are placed in ChatGPT for Teens. ChatGPT for Teachers is for verified U.S. K–12 school staff, not students.
  • Claude: Ages 18 and over. Claude for Teachers is for verified U.S. K–12 teachers, not students.

Rules differ between countries and change often. Check your own national rules and your school policy before students use any AI tool.
