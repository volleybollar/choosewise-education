Teach the Robot

One child plays a robot that learns "fruit" from two examples, then from eight, and sorts the same test cards both times. A toy apple shows that the robot guesses and people check.

At a glance

Ages
5–8
Time
34 min
Groups
Pairs, then the whole class
AI use

Preparation
20 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the Round 1 and Round 2 cards: one set per pair, cut out and in an envelope
  • Page 2, the four test cards: one set per pair, cut out and in a second envelope, and the two closing tickets
  • Page 3, the Fruit and Not fruit mat: one per pair
  • A toy apple, or a real carrot
  • Crayons or pencils
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain that a machine that learns from examples can only compare new things with the examples it was given.
  • Show that more examples, and more different ones, give better guesses.
  • Say why a person checks what a machine guesses.

Key words

example
Something you show to teach what a word means, like an apple card for fruit.
training
Showing a machine many examples, so that it can sort new things.
test
Checking how well something works on cards it was never shown.
guess
An answer that might be wrong. The robot's answers are guesses from its examples.

Before the lesson

About 20 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF and cut out the cards on pages 1 and 2 before the lesson, one set per pair. Put each pair's Round 1 and Round 2 cards in one envelope and the four test cards in another, so the test cards stay new to the child playing the robot. A paper trimmer helps.
  • Bring a toy apple, or other toy fruit from the play corner. If you have none, bring a real carrot: it looks most like the banana card, so the robot says fruit, and the class checks.
  • Read the background below, especially the robot's rule.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minAll

    The robot game

    Show the game with a volunteer at the front. In each pair, one child plays the robot and one the trainer. The robot may only compare a new card with the cards it has been shown: if it looks like a fruit card, it says fruit; if it looks like a not-fruit card, or like none of them, it says not fruit. The child playing the robot puts aside everything else they know about fruit.

    Say Robot, you can only look at the cards your trainer showed you.

  2. Step 23–9 minPairs

    Round 1

    Give each pair the two envelopes and the mat on page 3. The trainer shows the robot the two Round 1 cards, the apple and the cherry, and says fruit for each. Then the trainer takes out the four test cards one at a time, and the robot puts each one on the mat: Fruit or Not fruit.

    Say Robot, does this card look like one of your fruit cards?

  3. Step 39–12 minAll

    Round 1 on the board

    Draw the four test cards on the board in a row: lemon, avocado, tennis ball, cookie. For each card, ask the robots to show a thumb up if they put it on Fruit, and write the number of thumbs under it. Keep the answers for later.

    Say Robots, thumbs up if you put this card on Fruit.

  4. Step 412–20 minPairs

    Round 2

    Swap roles. The new trainer shows all eight Round 2 cards and says fruit or not fruit for each: the apple, the cherry, the banana and the melon are fruit; the ball, the coin, the pizza and the clock are not. The new robot follows the same rule, with only the Round 2 cards to compare with, and sorts the same four test cards again.

    Say Robot, which card does it look most like?

  5. Step 520–24 minAll

    Both rounds

    Count thumbs again under each card, this time for round 2. Then give the answers: the lemon and the avocado are fruit, the tennis ball and the cookie are not. Which cards moved to the right side? The tennis ball now looked like the ball and the cookie like the coin. Which cards are still wrong for some robots, and which card could we add to help?

    Say More examples, and different ones, helped with the ball and the cookie.

  6. Step 624–28 minAll

    The surprise

    Hold up the toy apple and ask the robots: fruit or not? It looks like the apple card, so the robot says fruit. Then ask the class whether we can eat it. It is a toy. The robot could only compare it with pictures; a person can pick it up and check. If you brought a carrot instead: the robot says fruit, because the carrot looks most like the banana card. Ask whether a carrot is a fruit. It is a vegetable, and a person checks.

    Say The robot says fruit. Can we eat it? A person checks.

  7. Step 728–31 minAll

    Who checks?

    Ask who chose the examples and who checked the guesses. People did both. Machines that learn from examples work in a similar way: people choose the examples, the machine compares, and people check what comes out.

    Say People choose the examples, and people check the guesses.

  8. Step 831–34 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Read the closing question aloud. Each child draws an answer on a ticket from page 2 and hands it in. Ask a few children why their card would help.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Why did the robot say the tennis ball was fruit?

    Students often say

    • It was round, like the apple.
    • It only had two fruit cards.
    • It didn't have a ball card to look at.

  • What made round 2 better?

    Students often say

    • More cards.
    • There were things that aren't fruit too.
    • The banana showed that fruit can be long.

  • Who should check the robot?

    Students often say

    • A grown-up.
    • Someone who can touch it.
    • The trainer.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Play both rounds as a whole class: you are the trainer, and one child at a time is the robot in front of everyone.

Harder

Let pairs choose four new Round 2 cards to make the robot better, or think up a tricky test card for another pair's robot and draw it on scrap paper.

Closing question

Draw one more card you would show the robot, so it guesses better.

Teaching in another language

Each card has one English word under a picture. Say the words in your class's language as you hand out pages 1 and 2, or write the translations on the cards before you cut them. On page 3, say Fruit and Not fruit in your class's language; the pictures above them help children who can't read yet. Read the closing question on page 2 aloud in your class's language.

Background for you

The robot game is a small version of how machine learning works. People collect examples and label them, a computer program finds a pattern in them, and then it is tested on examples it was not given before. In round 1 the robot has two examples, both round fruit, so everything round looks like fruit. Round 2 adds different fruit and round things that are not fruit, so the tennis ball and the cookie come out right. Some robots still get the lemon or the avocado wrong: that is the moment to ask which example would help.

The robot's rule is a simple form of a real method: a new example gets the label of the example it looks most like. Real systems compare far more features than shape, but the weakness is the same. A new thing that looks like an example gets that example's label, whether it fits or not. That is why the toy apple ends up as fruit.

The surprise is the main point at this age. The robot gives an answer for every card, including one unlike any it was shown, and the answer sounds the same either way. People choose the examples, and people check the guesses. Use words like compare and guess for what the robot does, not words for what goes on inside a person.
