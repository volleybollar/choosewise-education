The Word-Guessing Machine

The class builds a paper machine that writes sentences from word pairs, and finds out why a sentence can sound right and still be false.

At a glance

Ages
9–12
Time
40 min
Groups
Pairs, then the whole class
AI use
Teacher demo
Preparation
15 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the training text: one per pair, or show it on the projector
  • Page 2, the tally sheet, and page 3, the word slips: one of each per pair, and two for a pair that takes two words
  • Page 4, the sentence check and the closing question: one per pair
  • One cup, envelope or folded sheet per pair, to hold the word slips
  • Ten starting words, each on a scrap of paper
  • Scrap paper for the guesses in step 1
  • A board, and a projector for the demo
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain that a chatbot builds its answer by picking likely next words, based on patterns in the text it was built from.
  • Show with an example that a sentence can sound right and still be false.
  • Name one way to check something a chatbot wrote.

Key words

pattern
Something that happens again and again, like "is" often coming after "dog".
predict
To guess what comes next, from what has happened before.
training text
The text a machine's patterns come from. Our paper machine has one short text; a chatbot is built from billions of pages.
chatbot
A computer program that writes answers to the messages you type.
check
To find out whether something is true, for example in a book you trust or by asking a person who knows.

Before the lesson

About 15 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF. Page 3 needs no scissors: pairs fold and tear along the dashed lines.
  • Write the ten starting words on scraps of paper: the, my, is, can, in, dog, cat, sun, sea, bird. With more than ten pairs, give some words to two pairs; they can compare their counts. With fewer than ten pairs, some pairs take two words.
  • Draw the class chart on the board: one row for each starting word, with room for its next words and tally marks.
  • Choose a chatbot your school allows teachers to use, and try the demo prompts in step 9 on your own account. Search for the book title first to make sure no such book exists. Save a screenshot of any made-up answer you get, as a backup.
  • If your class does not read English well, write your own training text first (see Teaching in another language).

Step by step

  1. Step 10–2 minIndividual

    Your phone guesses too

    Write on the board: "See you after …". Ask how a phone keyboard comes up with the words it suggests next. Each student writes one guess, in one sentence, on scrap paper.

    Say When you type a message, the phone suggests the next word. How does it choose? Write down your best guess. We will come back to it at the end.

  2. Step 22–4 minAll

    Share the guesses

    Take three or four guesses and write a keyword from each in a corner of the board. Don't say which is right; leave the question open.

    Say Let's keep these. By the end of the lesson you can test them.

  3. Step 34–12 minPairs

    Count the next words

    Give each pair pages 1, 2 and 3 and a starting word. Pairs find their word every time it appears in the training text, write the word that comes right after it on the tally sheet, and make a tally mark each time. A full stop counts as a word. "The" and "the" are the same word. Pairs who finish early count again from the start, to check, and then start writing their slips (step 5) on the uncut page 3.

    Say Your pair is one part of the machine's memory. Find your word every time it appears. What comes right after it? Make a mark each time. If a full stop comes next, that counts too.

  4. Step 412–18 minAll

    Our machine's memory

    Each pair reads out its row, and you write it in the class chart, for example: dog → is ||, can ||. Ask which word comes most often after "the", and which next word would be picked most often if we drew one at random.

    Say This chart is everything our machine has. There are no real dogs or suns in it, only which word came after which.

  5. Step 518–21 minPairs

    Fill your cup

    Pairs write one slip for each tally mark on the uncut page 3: two marks for "is" means two slips that say "is". A full stop gets its own slip with a big dot. Then they tear off only the slips they wrote, fold them, put them in their cup and write their starting word on the cup. If two pairs counted the same word, they put their slips in one cup.

    Say One slip for every mark. Words that came often get more slips, so they get picked more often.

  6. Step 621–26 minAll

    Run the machine

    The class is now the machine. Say a first word: "My" works best; "The" often stops after two words. The pair with that cup draws one slip without looking, reads it aloud and puts it back. The pair whose cup is labelled with that word draws next. Stop at a full stop, or at a word that has no cup. A volunteer writes the sentence on the board. If a sentence stops after two words, start again. Make three sentences and number them 1 to 3.

    Say Draw without looking, read it out, put it back. Listen for your word!

  7. Step 726–28 minPairs

    Check the sentences

    On page 4, pairs tick for each sentence on the board: was it in the training text, or is it new? Is it true, false, or can't we tell?

  8. Step 828–32 minAll

    Sounds right, but is it true?

    Go through the three sentences. Which are new? Which sound right? Which are false, like "My cat can fly"? Ask where the words came from, and whether anything in the cups was about real cats, dogs or the sun. If students say the machine "knows" something, ask what we put in the cups. Then point at the guesses from step 1: which ones were close?

    Say Our machine picked words that often came next in the text. Nothing in it checked whether a sentence is true. A phone keyboard works in a similar way: it suggests likely next words, from patterns in text people have typed.

  9. Step 932–37 minAll

    A real chatbot

    Open the chatbot on the projector, on your own account; students don't type. Ask the class to predict what will happen, then type one of the prompts below. Read the answer aloud and ask: does it sound sure? Is it true? How could we check? Never type students' names or other personal details.

    Say A chatbot does what our machine did, with far more text: it picks likely next words, one after another. It can sound very sure and still be wrong.

    Prompts to try (pick one)

    • Who wrote the children's book Seven Socks for Mr Pebble, and how does it end?
    • Why is [the name of your town] famous for its purple sheep?
    • Tell me two facts about [a small street or park near the school], with the year each thing happened.

    If the demo does not go as planned: If the chatbot says it can't find anything, or answers correctly, say so: the people who build chatbots train them to say when they can't find a reliable answer, and that doesn't always work. Then ask the same thing twice, each time in a new chat: "Write one sentence about a dog in the sea." The two answers will usually differ, just like drawing from our cups. Ask the class: if the same question can get different answers, which one should we trust? If they come out the same, try a third time, or show the screenshot you saved when you tried the prompts.

  10. Step 1037–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Pairs tear off the closing questions at the bottom of page 4, one for each student. Each student answers on their own before leaving. Read the answers afterwards: who can explain why a sentence that sounds sure can still be false, and name a way to check?

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which of our sentences were already in the training text, and which were new?

    Students often say

    • Most of them were new.
    • "The sun is hot" was in the text.

  • Our machine made a sentence that is false. Why did that happen?

    Students often say

    • Because "can" came before "fly" in the text.
    • It just took a slip from the cup.
    • It doesn't know that cats can't fly.

  • What is the same about our paper machine and a chatbot? What is different?

    Students often say

    • Both pick the next word.
    • The chatbot has much more text.
    • The chatbot sounds cleverer, but it can still be wrong.

  • The chatbot sounded sure. How could we check whether it is right?

    Students often say

    • Look for the book in the library.
    • Ask someone who knows, like a librarian.
    • Search for it and see if real websites say the same.
    • Ask the chatbot again. (Point out that this is not checking: it can make up the same thing twice.)

  • Look at the guesses from the start of the lesson. Which ones were close?

    Students often say

    • It looks at what you usually write.
    • It guesses from lots of other people's messages.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Skip the counting. Fill in the class chart before the lesson; pairs copy their row onto slips and start at Fill your cup (step 5). Use the time you save to make more sentences and check them together.

Harder

Swap the cups for a die: share out its six faces by how often each next word came (for "dog": three faces "is", three faces "can"). Or look two words back: tally what comes after pairs of words, such as "my dog", run the machine again and compare. Do the sentences sound better? Are they true more often?

Closing question

A chatbot writes an answer that sounds very sure. Does that mean it is true? How could you check?

Teaching in another language

The training text is in simple English, but the lesson works in any language. Write 60 to 120 words in your class's language, like the example: short sentences about a few animals and places ("The dog is in the park."), using the same small words again and again. Pick about ten starting words that each appear at least twice, with different words after them, and check that no starting word appears more than 16 times, so that one page of slips is enough. Write your text on the board or hand it out instead of page 1. Pages 2 and 3 have only a few English words ("Our word", "Next word"): say them in your class's language. On page 4, read the column headings and the closing question aloud in your class's language, or write them on the board. Everything else in the lesson stays the same.

The English example (page 1 of the PDF)

My dog is big. My cat is small. My dog can run in the park. My cat can sleep in my bed. The bird can fly in the sky. The sun is hot. The sea is cold. The sea is blue. My dog can swim in the sea. The sun is in the sky. A bird is in the tree. My cat is in the sun. A fish can swim in the sea. My dog is in the park. My bird can fly.

Starting words: the, my, is, can, in, dog, cat, sun, sea, bird

Background for you

Chatbots run on large language models. A large language model is a computer program built from enormous amounts of text: books, websites and articles. During training, a computer measures which pieces of text tend to follow which, across billions of examples. The result is a huge set of numbers that holds those patterns.

When you type a question, the model produces its answer one small piece of text at a time. Each time, it calculates which pieces are likely to come next, given everything written so far, and picks one, with some chance built in. That is why the same question can get different answers.

The paper machine does the same thing in miniature. It looks one word back, and its memory is one short text. A real model looks thousands of words back and is built from far more text than a person could read in many lifetimes. After the first training, people tune it further by rating its answers, so that they come out helpful and polite.

Why fluent text can be false: nothing in this process compares a sentence with the real world. The model is built to produce text that looks like the text it came from, and confident, well-formed sentences are common in that text. So an answer can sound certain and be invented, such as a book that doesn't exist or a wrong date. Some people call these hallucinations; with students, "made-up answers" is clearer and doesn't make the machine sound like a person.

Many chatbots now search the web or add a warning when an answer may be unreliable, and that helps. It does not guarantee a true answer, so the habit to teach stays the same: if it matters, check it with a source you trust or a person who knows.
