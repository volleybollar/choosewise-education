Who Does It Fail?

An automatic door is built from ten pictures of people, and the class finds out who it leaves outside and how it could be fairer.

At a glance

Ages
9–12
Time
40 min
Groups
Pairs, then the whole class
AI use
No AI
Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the training pictures and the door's rule: one per pair (these cards stay on the page)
  • Page 2, the visitor cards and the door mat: one per pair, visitor cards cut out
  • Page 3, making it fairer and the closing questions: one per pair
  • The board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain that an AI system can work well for some people and badly for others, because of the examples it was built from.
  • Find who is missing from a set of examples, and who that leaves out.
  • Suggest changes that make a system fairer, including testing it with the people it affects.

Key words

training pictures
The examples a machine is built from. Our door has ten.
rule
What the training pictures have in common. The door opens when a visitor matches it.
bias
When a system works worse for some groups of people, often because they were missing from its examples.
fair
Working just as well for everyone it is meant for.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF and cut out the visitor cards on page 2, one set per pair. The training pictures on page 1 don't need cutting.
  • Read the background below. The real cases are optional in class: choose beforehand how much to tell, depending on your students.
  • Draw two columns on the board: The door opens, The door stays shut.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Who comes through our door?

    Each student writes down three different people who come in through the school's front door in a week.

    Say Think of a normal week. Who comes in through our front door? Write down three different people.

  2. Step 23–5 minAll

    Collect

    Collect answers on the board and make sure the list gets varied: students, teachers, parents, visitors, deliveries.

    Say So many different people use one door.

  3. Step 35–13 minPairs

    Train the door

    A company wants a door that opens only for people, and it built the door from the ten training pictures on page 1. Pairs look at all ten: what do they have in common? They write the door's rule at the bottom of page 1.

    Say The door has only these ten pictures. Whatever all ten have in common becomes the door's rule.

  4. Step 413–21 minPairs

    Visitors

    Pairs place each visitor card on the mat on page 2: does the door open, using only their rule? Remind them to use the rule, not what they think a good door should do.

    Say Use only your rule. Does this visitor match it?

  5. Step 521–29 minAll

    Who does it fail?

    Which visitors stayed outside? Collect them on the board and ask why: they were missing from the training pictures. Introduce the word bias. If it suits your class, tell one of the real cases from the background.

    Say The door works well for the people in its training pictures, and badly for everyone who was missing. Nobody meant to leave them out, but they were left out all the same.

  6. Step 629–37 minPairs

    Make it fairer

    On page 3, pairs write three pictures they would add to the training pictures, and one other change: for example, test the door with wheelchair users before it is used, or keep a button anyone can press. Collect a few ideas at the end.

    Say Who should the company ask before the door is put in?

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Pairs tear off the closing questions at the bottom of page 3, one for each student. Each student answers on their own before leaving.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which visitors did the door leave outside, and why?

    Students often say

    • The wheelchair, because nobody in the pictures sits.
    • The small child, because everyone in the pictures is tall.
    • The person with the box, you can't see their legs.

  • Did the company want to leave anyone out?

    Students often say

    • No, they just forgot.
    • They only took pictures of people like themselves.

  • What would make the door fairer?

    Students often say

    • More different pictures.
    • Ask people in wheelchairs to test it.
    • A button that always works.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Show the training pictures on the projector and work out the rule together as a class. Then pairs only sort the visitors.

Harder

Ask pairs to write a rule that lets every visitor in, then test it: does the door now also open for a dog on its own, a delivery robot or a shopping trolley? Which is worse for this door: staying shut for a person, or opening for something that isn't one?

Closing question

Who might an AI system leave out if it only learns from examples of some people? What could its makers do?

Teaching in another language

The cards have short English phrases under pictures, and the pictures carry most of the meaning. On page 1, read the ten training phrases aloud in your class's language, or translate them on the board. On page 2, say each visitor phrase and the two mat headings in your class's language. On page 3, say the headings and the closing question aloud, or write them on the board.

Background for you

An AI system built from examples works best for the kinds of cases it had many examples of. When some people are missing or rare in the examples, the system tends to make more mistakes for them. Nobody needs to intend this for it to happen: it follows from the data, and it can go unnoticed if the system is only tested on the same kinds of people.

A well-known study, Gender Shades (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018), tested commercial systems that guess gender from face photos. For lighter-skinned men the error rate was at most 0.8 %; for darker-skinned women it was up to 34.7 %. The face collections these systems were built and tested on had far more lighter-skinned faces.

A simpler case shows the same pattern without any AI. In 2017 a video of an automatic soap dispenser went viral: it gave soap to a white hand but not to a dark one. Reports blamed an infrared sensor that reacts to reflected light. Whatever the exact cause, the device had not been tested on enough different hands.

Our door is made up, but the mechanism is real. The training pictures show only adults walking upright, so the rule students find ("a tall person walking on two legs") leaves out wheelchair users, small children, people with prams or crutches, and more. Fixing it takes more varied examples, testing with the people affected, and sometimes a way around the system, such as a button.

Talk about the real cases calmly and briefly: the point is the mechanism, not blame. Some students may have been left out by technology themselves, so let them choose whether to share.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

If students mention apps or chatbots they use at home, most of them have a minimum age of 13 on a personal account.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
