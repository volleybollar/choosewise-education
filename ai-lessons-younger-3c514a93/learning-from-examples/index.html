Learning from Examples

Students treat a pile of picture cards as a dataset: they check it, label it, write a rule and watch the rule fail on new examples.

At a glance

Materials

  • Student PDF pages 1 and 2, the 20 dataset cards and two blank cards: one set per pair, cut out
  • Page 3, the six new cards: one set per pair, cut out by you and kept in an envelope
  • Page 4, the sorting mat, the rule and the closing questions: one per pair
  • The board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Explain that many AI systems are built from a dataset: examples that people collect, check and label.
  • Spot a dataset that is missing examples, or has examples that don't belong.
  • Show how a rule found in examples can fail on a new case, and what people do about it.

Key words

dataset
A collection of examples, like our 20 picture cards.
label
A name we give each example, like land, water or air.
rule
A way to tell which group something belongs to, like "it has wheels".
biased
When a dataset leaves out important examples, or has far more of one kind, so the results come out lopsided or wrong.
test
To try a rule on examples it was not made from.

Before the lesson

About 25 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF and cut out the cards on pages 1 and 2 before the lesson, one set per pair. A paper trimmer helps; cutting in class takes pairs five minutes or more. Keep the sets for your next class.
  • Cut out page 3 yourself and put each pair's six new cards in an envelope, so students don't see them until step 5. The train is among them on purpose.
  • Notice what is there on purpose: there is no train among the 20 dataset cards, and three cards don't belong (a banana, an armchair and a cloud).
  • Draw three columns on the board: Land, Water, Air.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minPairs

    What is this dataset?

    Pairs spread out their 20 cards and agree on what most of them show. Then they put aside the cards that don't belong.

    Say Look at all your cards. What do most of them show? Are there any that don't belong?

  2. Step 23–6 minAll

    A dataset

    Collect the answer (ways to travel) and the cards that don't belong. Explain that a set of examples like this is called a dataset, and that people build it and check it.

    Say A computer that is built from these cards would count the banana as a way to travel, unless a person takes it out. Nothing in the computer checks that by itself.

  3. Step 36–14 minPairs

    Land, water or air?

    Pairs sort their cards on the mat (page 4) into Land, Water and Air. Then they look for a way to travel that is missing and draw it on one of the blank cards.

    Say Is anything missing? If a way to travel isn't in our cards, a machine built from them has no example of it.

  4. Step 414–18 minAll

    What was missing?

    Collect the missing ways to travel; usually someone spots that there is no train. Introduce the word biased: a dataset that leaves out important examples gives a lopsided picture.

    Say A machine built from our cards would have no example of a train. Its guesses can only be as good as its examples.

  5. Step 518–28 minPairs

    Write a rule

    Each pair writes a rule for Land on page 4 (many write "it has wheels") and tests it on all their cards: does the rule put every card in the right group? Then hand out the envelopes; pairs open them and sort the six new cards using only their rule.

    Say A machine can only use the rule it found in its examples. Use only your rule, even if you know better.

  6. Step 628–37 minAll

    Fix the rule

    Which new cards did the rules get wrong? The sleigh and the skis have no wheels but travel on land, the plane has wheels but flies, and the hovercraft travels on land and on water. Improve one rule together on the board and test it again. Then connect it to AI: systems find rules in examples and can fail on cases unlike their examples; people test them on new examples and add more varied ones.

    Say The people who build AI systems do this too: they test with new examples, find the mistakes, and add the examples that were missing.

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Pairs tear off the closing questions at the bottom of page 4, one for each student. Each student answers on their own before leaving.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Why did we take out the banana, the armchair and the cloud?

    Students often say

    • They aren't ways to travel.
    • The machine would learn the wrong thing.

  • Many rules said "it has wheels". Which cards did that rule get wrong?

    Students often say

    • The sleigh, it has no wheels.
    • The plane has wheels, but it flies.
    • The hovercraft goes on water too.

  • What should the people who build a machine like this do?

    Students often say

    • Add more examples.
    • Test it on new things.
    • Ask other people what's missing.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Use only the land and water cards, and give pairs the rule "it has wheels" instead of writing their own. Then test it on the new cards together.

Harder

Let pairs write rules for all three groups, swap rules with another pair, and test them on the new cards: which rule breaks first? Or ask: if a real AI system learned from photos of vehicles from only one country, which vehicles might it get wrong?

Closing question

Why does a machine that learns from examples need many different examples? Use one card from today.

Teaching in another language

Each card has one English word under a picture. Say the words in your class's language as you hand out pages 1 and 2, or write the translations on the cards before you cut them; do the same for the new cards on page 3. On page 4, write Land, Water and Air and the rule frame in your class's language, and say the closing question aloud or write it on the board.

Background for you

Many AI systems are built from examples rather than from rules people write. To build a system that recognises vehicles in photos, people collect thousands of photos, label each one (car, boat, plane) and let a computer find patterns that separate the labels. The collection is called a dataset. The patterns work like the system's rules, although nobody writes them down.

What the system can do depends on the dataset. If trains are missing, the system has no train examples and will put a train in some other group. If a banana is labelled as a vehicle, that mistake becomes part of the patterns too. Checking a dataset for gaps and errors is ordinary work in AI, and people do it: nothing in the computer can tell that a label is wrong.

A dataset that leaves out important examples, or has far more of one kind than another, is often called biased. The result is a system that works well for what it had many examples of and badly for the rest. Lesson 3 takes this a step further: what happens when the missing examples are people?

Students' rules, such as "it has wheels", behave like the patterns a learning system finds: they work on the examples they came from and fail on new cases, such as a sleigh or a hovercraft. Real systems are tested on examples that were kept out of the training for exactly this reason. When they fail, people add more varied examples and test again; the work is never quite finished.

The original activity uses about 70 picture cards over several lessons. This version uses 20 cards plus six new ones so that it fits 40 minutes, and the set is incomplete on purpose so that students find the gaps themselves.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

If students mention apps or chatbots they use at home, most of them have a minimum age of 13 on a personal account.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
