Who Decides?

Students work out who chooses what apps show them, spot chatbot messages that mean asking an adult, and write rules for a chatbot for children.

At a glance

Ages
9–12
Time
40 min
Groups
Pairs, then the whole class

Preparation
10 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the "Who chooses?" grid: one per pair
  • Page 2, four chatbot messages and the rule frame: one per pair
  • Page 3, three tricky-message cards and the closing questions: one per pair, cards cut out
  • The board, and a large sheet of paper for the class poster
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Tell when they choose something themselves, and when an app or another person chooses it for them.
  • Recognise chatbot messages that mean: stop and ask an adult.
  • Write rules for a chatbot for children, and test them on tricky messages.

Key words

recommendation
Something an app suggests to you, like the next video, picked from patterns in what you and others watched.
company
The people who make and own an app. They choose what the app is for.
personal information
Things about you, like your address, your password or photos of you.
rule
Something a chatbot must always or never do, written by the people who build it.

Before the lesson

About 10 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF and cut out the three tricky-message cards on page 3, one set per pair.
  • Prepare a large sheet of paper titled "Our rules for a chatbot for children" for the class poster.
  • Read the background below. If your school has rules for apps and AI, have them at hand: students often ask.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    Who chose it?

    Each student thinks of the last video, song or game an app showed them, and writes down who chose it: they did, the app did, or someone else.

    Say Think of the last video, song or game an app showed you. Who chose it? Write it down.

  2. Step 23–5 minAll

    Share

    Take three or four answers. Many will say the app chose; ask how.

    Say So sometimes we choose, and sometimes an app chooses for us.

  3. Step 35–13 minPairs

    Who chooses?

    Pairs fill in the grid on page 1: for each of the eight situations, who chooses? Me, the app, another person, or not sure. More than one tick is allowed.

    Say For each situation, ask: who is really choosing here?

  4. Step 413–19 minAll

    How apps choose

    Go through the situations where pairs disagreed. Explain that an app picks what to show from patterns in what you and people like you did before, and that the company behind the app chooses what it is for, for example to keep you watching or to show more ads. Then ask: who should choose what you watch next?

    Say The app doesn't choose on its own. People at a company chose what it should aim for, and the app picks from patterns in what millions of people did.

  5. Step 519–24 minPairs

    Ask an adult?

    Pairs read the four chatbot messages on page 2 and tick for each one: stop and ask an adult, or fine to answer? They write one word about why.

    Say Would you answer this message, or stop and ask an adult first?

  6. Step 624–34 minPairs

    Rules for a chatbot

    Pairs write four rules for a chatbot for children on page 2, using the sentence starters. Then they swap with another pair, take one tricky-message card from page 3 and check: do the other pair's rules say what the chatbot should do?

    Say Imagine you build a chatbot for children your age. What must it always do? What must it never do?

  7. Step 734–37 minAll

    Our poster

    Each pair reads out its best rule. The class picks five or six for the poster.

  8. Step 837–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Pairs tear off the closing questions at the bottom of page 3, one for each student. Each student answers on their own before leaving.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which situations did you disagree about?

    Students often say

    • The TV, because my family chooses together.
    • The keyboard, because I pick the word but it suggests it.

  • Why does an app want you to keep watching?

    Students often say

    • So it can show you more ads.
    • So you like the app more.

  • Which chatbot message would you never answer?

    Students often say

    • The password one.
    • The one that says don't tell your parents.

  • What makes a good rule for a chatbot?

    Students often say

    • It's clear.
    • It keeps kids safe.
    • It says it's not a person.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Use four situations instead of eight, and write the chatbot rules together as a class on the board.

Harder

Ask pairs to put their rules in order of importance, and to find a message where two of their rules clash. Or look at the rules your school already has for apps and AI: what would the class add?

Closing question

Name one thing an app chooses for you, and one thing you want to choose yourself.

Teaching in another language

Read the eight situations on page 1 aloud in your class's language, or write them on the board; the four column headings are short words to translate once. On page 2, read the four chatbot messages and the sentence starters aloud in your class's language, and let students write their rules in it. On page 3, read the tricky messages aloud, and say the closing question in your class's language or write it on the board.

Background for you

Many apps choose what you see next: the next video, the songs on a playlist, the friends a game suggests. These recommendation systems pick from patterns in what you did before and what people with similar habits did. They are built by companies, and the company chooses what the system should aim for, often to keep you watching or to show more ads. That choice matters more than any single recommendation.

For children the useful question is not "is this app good or bad?" but "who chose this, and why?". Sometimes they choose, sometimes another person does, sometimes an app does, and often it is a mix. Seeing the mix is the first step to choosing for themselves.

Chatbot messages raise other questions. A chatbot that asks for an address or a password, says it is your best friend, or asks you to keep secrets from adults shows the same warning signs as any stranger online. The simple rule at this age: if a chatbot or an app asks for personal information or secrets, stop and ask an adult.

Writing rules for a chatbot is what the companies behind real chatbots do too: they write instructions and policies for what their systems must and must not do, and test them with tricky messages. Students' rules will be simpler, but the method is the same: write a rule, test it on a hard case, improve it.

Rules for children and apps differ between countries, and many chatbots and social apps have a minimum age of 13. If your school or country has its own rules, this is a good lesson to bring them in.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

Most chatbots and many apps have a minimum age of 13 on a personal account; students who use them at home may want to talk about that.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
