AI or Not?

Students sort everyday machines into those that always do the same and those that find patterns and guess, and the class writes its own definition of AI.

At a glance

Ages
9–12
Time
40 min
Groups
Pairs, then the whole class
AI use
No AI
Preparation
20 min

Materials

  • Student PDF page 1, the 16 picture cards: one set per pair, cut out
  • Page 2, the sorting mat: one per pair
  • Page 3, the definition frame and the closing questions: one per pair
  • Scrap paper or sticky notes, and the board
Download the student PDF

Learning goals

By the end of the lesson, students can:

  • Tell the difference between a machine that follows fixed rules and an AI system that finds patterns in examples and makes guesses.
  • Give two examples of AI in everyday life and say how they can tell.
  • Describe what a voice assistant does in precise words, without making it sound like a person.

Key words

rule
An exact instruction written by a person: "If the button is pressed, turn on the light."
pattern
Something that happens again and again, which a computer can find in lots of examples.
AI (artificial intelligence)
Computer programs that find patterns in lots of examples and use them to guess, sort or suggest things.
voice assistant
A computer program you talk to, on a phone or a smart speaker. It turns your voice into words and picks a reply.

Before the lesson

About 20 minutes.

  • Print the student PDF and cut out the cards before the lesson, one set per pair, with an envelope per set. A paper trimmer helps; cutting in class takes pairs five minutes or more. Keep the sets for your next class.
  • Write the two test questions on the board: "Do the same thing twice: is the result always the same?" and "Did people write every rule, or did it find patterns in lots of examples?"
  • Read the background below, especially the part about the two tricky cards, the robot vacuum and the heating.

Step by step

  1. Step 10–3 minIndividual

    What counts as AI?

    Each student writes two things on scrap paper: one thing they think uses AI, and one thing they think doesn't.

    Say Write one thing you think uses AI and one thing you think doesn't. There are no wrong answers yet.

  2. Step 23–5 minAll

    Two lists

    Take four or five answers and write them in two lists on the board: AI and not AI. Don't correct anything yet; the lists are checked in step 6.

    Say Let's keep these. We will check them against what we find out.

  3. Step 35–16 minPairs

    Same every time, or a guess?

    Give each pair a set of cards and the sorting mat. Pairs place each card in one of three columns: Always does the same, Finds patterns and guesses, or Not sure. Point at the two test questions on the board. Pairs who finish early pick the card they argued about most.

    Say Look at each card and ask the two questions on the board. If you can't agree, put the card under Not sure.

  4. Step 416–24 minAll

    Where we disagree

    Pairs compare their mat with the pair next to them. Then take the cards that ended up in different columns around the room, and let pairs explain their choice. Settle each one with the test questions. The robot vacuum and the heating may stay under Not sure: some models find patterns, others only follow rules.

    Say A calculator gives the same answer every time, because people wrote every rule. A music app suggests different songs to different people, because it finds patterns in what millions of people listen to.

  5. Step 524–29 minPairs

    Does it have feelings?

    Read the exchange below aloud twice, then let pairs talk about the two questions. If a pair says the voice assistant feels sorry, ask: what in the machine could do the feeling? What happened instead?

    Say A child says: "I'm sad today." The voice assistant answers: "I'm sorry to hear that. Would you like to hear a joke?" Does it feel sorry? What happened instead?

  6. Step 629–37 minAll

    Our definition

    Build the class's definition with the frame on page 3: "AI is a … that … from … and …". Collect words for each gap, vote, and write the result on the board; pairs copy it onto page 3. Then go back to the two lists from step 2: does anything need to move?

    Say Choose words we can check, like patterns, examples and guess, and not words that make it sound like a person.

  7. Step 737–40 minIndividual

    Closing question

    Pairs tear off the two closing questions at the bottom of page 3, one for each student. Each student answers on their own before leaving.

Gather it up

Questions for the whole-class talk, with answers students often give.

  • Which card was hardest to place, and why?

    Students often say

    • The robot vacuum, because some just bump around and some learn the room.
    • The traffic light, because some change when cars come.

  • Did the voice assistant feel sorry?

    Students often say

    • No, it just says what it was made to say.
    • It sounds like it cares.
    • Maybe a little bit?

  • Is everything that is called smart AI?

    Students often say

    • No, a smart lamp just turns on from your phone.
    • If it learns, it's AI.

  • What would you move in the two lists from the start of the lesson?

    Students often say

    • The calculator isn't AI.
    • The spam filter is AI.

Make it simpler, make it harder

Simpler

Use eight cards, four clear examples from each side, and leave out the Not sure column. Write the definition together on the board instead of on page 3.

Harder

Add a fourth column: Finds patterns, but a person has the final say. Ask pairs which card belongs there (for example, a spam filter that puts mail in a folder you can check). Or let each pair invent a card for something in the classroom and place it.

Closing question

Name one thing at home or at school that uses AI. How can you tell?

Teaching in another language

Each card has one English word or phrase under a picture. Say the words in your class's language as you hand out page 1, or write the translations on the cards before you cut them. On page 2, write the three column headings in your class's language. On page 3, say the definition frame and the closing question aloud in your class's language, or write them on the board.

Background for you

There is no single agreed definition of AI. The OECD's definition, updated in 2023, describes an AI system as a machine-based system that infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. The EU AI Act builds on the same wording. For a class of ten-year-olds the useful word is infers: the system was not given a rule for every case, but works out an output from patterns.

Most of these systems are built with machine learning. People collect lots of examples (songs people played, emails marked as spam, photos of faces), and a computer finds patterns in them. The result is used to guess about new cases: which song you might like, or whether a new email is spam. Because the patterns come from examples, the output can differ from person to person, and it can be wrong.

A calculator, a light switch or a lift is different: people wrote every rule, and the same input always gives the same output. That is why the two test questions work in class. They are not a perfect test (some AI systems are built to give the same answer every time, and some ordinary programs use chance), but they sort the cards well enough at this age.

The tricky cards are tricky on purpose. A cheap robot vacuum bumps around by fixed rules; others map the room and adjust their route. Heating that learns when you are home finds patterns in when you come and go; a simple timer does not. Let the class leave them under Not sure: the question matters more than the verdict. And "smart" on a product does not mean it uses AI.

A voice assistant turns speech into text, matches the text to the request it most likely is, and picks or produces a reply. A kind reply to "I'm sad" was written by people or produced from patterns in human writing; nothing in the device feels sorry. A study of children aged 5–12 found that many think voice assistants are smarter than they are and are not sure whether they have feelings. That is why this lesson practises precise words.

AI tools and age limits

This lesson uses no AI tool. Students don't use one, and you don't show one.

If students name AI apps they use at home, remember that most of them have a minimum age of 13 on a personal account.

Readers outside Sweden should verify their own national regulations. AI in education is evolving quickly and country rules differ significantly.
