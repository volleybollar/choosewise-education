What teachers do

Teachers have too little time, and new teachers also lack experience. This package goes through the eleven tasks that fill a teacher's week, shows where the time and the stress sit, and gives each task a way to do it with less work and teaching that is as good or better.

Across all school stages

US K–12 public school teachers reported a typical week of 49 hours in 2025, ten more than their 39 contracted hours and five more than similar working adults (RAND 2025). Federal data from 2020–21 give 52 hours, 25 of them paid teaching, against a 38-hour contract (NCES 2022). Teachers in England reported 46.9 hours in spring 2025, 50.1 for full-time teachers (DfE).

These surveys cover all school stages, and the US ones ask about a typical week; TALIS asks about the last week in lower secondary, so the totals differ.

  • England: 71% say administration takes too much time, and 50% say the same about parents (DfE, spring 2025).
  • United States: 55% of teachers report frequent job stress, against 34% of similar working adults. After student behaviour, the most common top stressors are low pay (33%), too many hours outside contract time (32%) and administrative work (32%) (RAND 2026). Asked what besides pay would most improve their morale, the top answer was more planning time in the school day (54%) (EdWeek 2026).
  • New teachers: in Sweden, 55% of novices find keeping order stressful, against 35% of experienced teachers (TALIS 2024).

Planning takes the most time outside lessons, but of the tasks above it stresses the fewest teachers. Administration stresses most everywhere; in Sweden, marking and adapting for different needs come next.

What AI has been shown to save

Two randomised trials have measured it, and both are about lesson planning. In a trial of ChatGPT with Year 7–8 science teachers in England, planning time fell by 31%, about 25 min a week. In a trial of Oak's Aila with Years 3–6 teachers, it fell by 24%, about 49 min a week. The quality of the planned lessons and materials was not lower in either trial.

Teachers' own estimates are much larger. In Gallup's 2025 survey, US teachers who use AI weekly estimate that it saves them 5.9 hours a week. That is a self-report across all tasks, and it covers only the 32% of teachers who use AI every week, so it cannot be set against the trials.

We have found no controlled study of AI for marking, feedback, reports or parent contact among school teachers.

Checking AI output is work too. Interviews with 57 teachers in Sweden and Australia (Selwyn, Ljungqvist and Sonesson, 2025, as reported in the OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026) describe the hidden labour of reviewing and repairing AI drafts for administrative tasks, time that estimates may not fully count.

So this package promises minutes, not hours. The biggest gains for the most stressful tasks probably come from cutting work, not from automating it.

Three rules for every part

  1. No student names and no student work in any prompt. Names and details are added afterwards, outside AI.
  2. You decide first, AI drafts after, and you check everything before it reaches students.
  3. Cut before you automate. Work that does not help students is better removed than done faster.
