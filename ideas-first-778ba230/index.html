Ideas first

AI makes ideas easy to get, and they tend to look alike. Write your own ideas first, ask for many that differ on purpose, then choose one and make it yours. No student details needed.

Four steps

  1. Write your own two or three ideas before you ask.
  2. Ask for many ideas that differ on purpose, in format, context and level of challenge. Not for "the best idea".
  3. Choose one and change it to fit your class. Not the first one you see.
  4. In a team, collect everyone's ideas before anyone uses AI.

Framework The four steps are our reading of the studies below. None of the studies on ideas is of teachers planning lessons.

Choose and change

This step is yours, without AI. It is where an idea becomes your lesson.

Check before use

  1. Are the eight really different, or one activity in eight wordings? Ask again for the kind that is missing.

  2. Does the idea you chose serve the goal, and is the subject content right?
  3. What did you change? If you cannot say, you took the AI's idea, not your own.

Three ways to change an idea

  • Make it local: your own town, your school, a place your class knows.
  • Combine it with one of your own ideas.
  • Fit the level of challenge to your class.

(our suggestion, not a research finding)

In a team

When everyone asks the same tool the same question, a department can end up teaching the same AI lesson. In the story study, AI helped individual writers, but the stories became more alike. A short routine keeps everyone's own ideas in play.

  1. Agree on the goal in one sentence.
  2. Everyone writes two or three ideas alone, before anyone talks and before anyone uses AI (five minutes).
  3. Read them out and collect them on one sheet.
  4. One person chooses "Our team" in Get ideas, pastes everyone's ideas and runs the prompt. Read the eight together.
  5. Each teacher, or the team together, chooses one and writes down what they changed.

About fifteen minutes in all. The timings are our suggestion, not a research finding; ideas before AI is the Framework step.

What the research says

The studies on ideas point the same way: with AI, ideas become more alike, even where individual ideas get better. None of the studies on ideas is of teachers planning lessons; the one trial with teachers measured time, not variety.

Promising the approach as a whole

  • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that working this way improves learning.
  • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
  • Framework A useful structure. The method itself has not been tested in intervention studies.
  • Choosewise Our own rule, not a research finding.

More creative, less varied In an online experiment, adults who got story ideas from an AI wrote stories judged more creative, better written and more enjoyable, with the largest gains among the less creative writers. But the stories were more similar to each other: individual writers gained, and the range across writers narrowed. Source: Doshi & Hauser 2024, Science Advances

Across 28 studies A meta-analysis of 28 studies with 8,214 participants found people working with generative AI more creative than people without it (0.27, a small effect), while the diversity of their ideas fell (−0.86, a large drop). AI alone and people alone did not differ (−0.05). It is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. Source: Holzner, Maier & Feuerriegel 2025, arXiv preprint

Less distinct, less owned In a study with 36 participants, people using ChatGPT produced more and more detailed ideas, but the ideas from different users were less distinct, and the users "felt less responsible for the ideas they generated". Source: Anderson, Shah & Kreminski 2024, Creativity and Cognition

Stuck on the first example In a design study with 60 participants, an AI image generator increased fixation on the first example. Participants produced fewer ideas, with less variety and lower originality. Source: Wadinambiarachchi et al. 2024, CHI '24

The one trial with teachers In a trial in England, science teachers of 11- to 14-year-olds who used ChatGPT with a short guide spent 56.2 minutes a week on lesson and resource preparation, against 81.5 minutes in the comparison group (31% less). An expert panel found that the quality "did not appear to be affected". The trial measured time, not how varied the ideas were. Source: Roy et al. 2024, ChatGPT in lesson preparation (EEF/NFER), summary by Stanford SCALE

Framework The four steps Write your own ideas first, ask for many that differ on purpose, choose one and change it, and in a team collect everyone's ideas before anyone uses AI. This is our reading of the studies above, not a tested method.

Watch out for

  • One activity in eight wordings Copy the follow-up prompt and ask for the kinds that are missing.
  • Wrong subject content Check every fact before you use an idea, as with any AI text.
  • Taking the first idea Read all eight before you choose, and write down what you changed.
  • The whole department teaching the same AI lesson Collect your own ideas first and use "Our team" in Get ideas.
  • A student becomes identifiable in the "must fit" line Describe the lesson or the class as a whole, never one student.
