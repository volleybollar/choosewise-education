Thinking Profile

Thinking Profile helps you plan a whole unit around one kind of thinking, so students practise it in four to six tasks and can see for themselves that they are getting better. Pick one kind of thinking for the unit, plan four to six tasks around it, and let students rate every answer on the same four-step ladder.

One cluster per unit

Generating ideas
Producing many and unusual options.
Evaluating and choosing
Weighing evidence, testing claims and deciding.
Comparing and transferring
Seeing the structure one case shares with another.
Explaining and reasoning from basics
Finding the best explanation and rebuilding from what is certainly true.
Causes and systems
Chains, loops and side effects.
Regulating your own thinking
Watching and adjusting how you think. It makes every other cluster sharper.

After every task, students ask

1. Which step did my answer reach?
2. What in my answer shows it?
3. My next step

The ladder is for students' own self-check. It is never graded.

The ladders are Choosewise's own, built on the logic of the SOLO taxonomy. They have not been tested in research.

What the labels mean

Strong Meta-analyses of classroom interventions show that teaching this kind of thinking improves it.

Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.

Framework A useful structure for thinking, with no intervention studies behind it yet.

Cluster 1

Generating ideas

Producing many and unusual options.

1

I write down one idea that works.

Shows it: Point to the idea and to what makes it work.

2

I write down several ideas that work in different ways, not small changes of the same idea.

Shows it: Point to each idea and to what makes it different from the others.

3

I sort my ideas into kinds and add one from a kind I had missed.

Shows it: Point to your groups, their names and the idea you added to fill the gap.

4

I pick the idea that fits best when one condition changes, explain why, and say how sure I am.

Shows it: Point to the changed condition, the idea you picked, your reason and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

Students solve one problem in as many different ways as they can, then hunt for the most unusual way.

Cluster 2

Evaluating and choosing

Weighing evidence, testing claims and deciding.

1

I make a choice or a judgement and give one reason for it.

Shows it: Point to your choice and the one reason.

2

I give several reasons, each backed by a fact or a rule I can check.

Shows it: Point to each reason and the fact or rule beside it.

3

I weigh my reasons against each other and against the other side, and show which one decides it.

Shows it: Point to where you answer the other side and the sentence that says which reason weighs most.

4

I test my judgement on a new case or a new piece of evidence, and say how sure I am.

Shows it: Point to the new case or evidence, whether your judgement held, and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

Students set criteria or say what evidence would convince them before they decide, then defend their choice against the other side.

Cluster 3

Comparing and transferring

Seeing the structure one case shares with another.

1

I name one thing the two cases share.

Shows it: Point to the shared feature in both cases.

2

I name several things that match and at least one place where the cases differ.

Shows it: Point to each match and to the difference.

3

I explain the rule that makes the cases match, and where the comparison breaks.

Shows it: Point to the sentence that states the rule and to the place where the comparison breaks.

4

I use the rule on a new case, and say how sure I am that it fits.

Shows it: Point to the new case, your rule used on it, and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

Students set two cases side by side, map what matches, write the rule the comparison shows and try it on a new case.

Cluster 4

Explaining and reasoning from basics

Finding the best explanation and rebuilding from what is certainly true.

1

I give one explanation or one fact I know for certain.

Shows it: Point to the explanation or the fact.

2

I give several explanations or steps, and say what each one rests on.

Shows it: Point to each explanation or step and what it rests on: evidence, a known fact or an assumption.

3

I link my steps into one line of reasoning, or show which explanation fits the evidence best.

Shows it: Point to the linking words (because, so, which means) or to the evidence that rules out the other explanations.

4

I use my reasoning on a new case or a changed number, say where it stops working, and say how sure I am.

Shows it: Point to the new case, the place where your reasoning stops working, and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

Students explain a puzzling result, rebuild a rule from what they know for certain, or estimate and model a real question, saying every assumption out loud.

Cluster 5

Causes and systems

Chains, loops and side effects.

1

I name one cause and one effect.

Shows it: Point to the cause, the effect and the word that links them.

2

I build a chain: A leads to B leads to C.

Shows it: Point to each link in the chain: at least three parts in a row.

3

I find a loop: C affects A again, or a delay or a side effect.

Shows it: Point to the arrow or sentence that leads back to the start, or that names the delay or side effect.

4

I use the loop to predict what happens if one part changes, and say how sure I am.

Shows it: Point to the part you changed, your prediction and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

Students follow a decision through its consequences, or draw how the parts of a system affect each other and find the loops.

Cluster 6

Regulating your own thinking

Watching and adjusting how you think. It makes every other cluster sharper.

1

I name one thing I did while I worked.

Shows it: Point to the strategy you name, such as "I drew it". "I thought harder" does not count.

2

I describe several steps in how I worked, and one moment that shaped how I worked, such as changing my approach or getting stuck.

Shows it: Point to each step and to the moment that shaped how you worked.

3

I explain what made me change my approach or moved me on, and why it helped.

Shows it: Point to the sentence that says what made you change your approach or moved you on, and why it helped.

4

I say what I would change next time and why, and how sure I am that it would help.

Shows it: Point to what you would change, your reason and the words that say how sure you are.

In class

At the end of any task, students write for two minutes about how they thought, not what they found, and what they would do differently.

Worked example

Biology, Grade 8: Ecosystems

6 weeks · following Causes and systems

The thinking thread

One question runs through the unit: when one thing in an ecosystem changes, what else changes, and when? Each week students follow the effects a little further: a chain in week 1, a loop in week 2, a delayed side effect in week 3, and by week 6 a prediction they back with the loop.

Week
Task
What students do
Room for
Week 1
Then what?
Queensland, 1935: cane toads are released to eat the beetles that damage sugar cane. Students write the first consequence, the consequence of that, and one more.
Step 2
Week 2
Map the loop
Students draw how snowshoe hares, lynx and the shrubs the hares eat affect each other, find one loop and say whether it grows or balances itself.
Step 3
Week 3
Then what?
Harder inputs: a farmer spreads extra fertiliser next to a lake. Students follow the chain into the water and find the delayed side effect: algae bloom, die and rot, and the fish run short of oxygen.
Step 3
Week 4
Best explanation
Borrowed from Explaining. 29 reindeer released on St. Matthew Island in 1944 grew to about 6,000 by 1963, then fell to 42 by 1966. Students propose explanations and say what evidence would tell them apart. Researchers still disagree: overgrazed lichen plus a hard winter, or the winter alone.
Step 3
Week 5
Side by Side: One thing changed
Borrowed from Comparing. Two identical forests have too many deer, and both remove the same number every year. In A most of the deer removed are females, in B most are males. Students compare what happens, write the rule and predict a third forest.
Step 4
Week 6
Map the loop
Back to the hares, now with less support: a disease halves the lynx. Students use their loop to predict what happens to hares and shrubs over the next few years, and say how sure they are.
Step 4
Tell students

For six weeks we practise one kind of thinking: causes and systems. After every task you check your answer against the same four-step ladder, point to the words that show your step, and write your next step. Nobody grades the ladder. It is there so you can see yourself climb.
