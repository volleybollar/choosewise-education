Side by Side

Use any AI tool to build two items placed side by side: two solutions, cases, concepts or sources. Students compare them, write the rule they found and try it on a new case, in about fifteen minutes.

Four steps

Compare
Fill in the three columns: only in A, in both, only in B.
Write the rule
In one or two sentences, say what the comparison shows.
Try it on case C
Use your rule on the new case and explain your answer.
Think back
Finish the sentence about how you thought, not what you found.

Two levels

Easier to see
A and B differ in one difference students can spot at once. The work is to explain why that difference matters.
Harder to see
The surface and the principle do not line up: A and B look almost the same but rest on different principles, or look different but share one principle.

Copy only the FOR STUDENTS block for students. Before you print, read the TEACHER KEY and check everything listed under CHECK. Let students write the rule before you confirm it.

Pair 1

Two ways to the same answer

Two correct methods reach the same answer by different routes.

What to look for
  • Where do the two methods first part ways?
  • What does each method save, and what does it cost?
  • When would you choose each one?

Maths · Grade 8 · Easier to see

Task: Solve 3(x + 2) = 15.

A · Alex3x + 6 = 15
3x = 9
x = 3
B · Samx + 2 = 5
x = 3
Only AAlex multiplies out the brackets first, then undoes the + 6 and the × 3. Three lines.
BothThe same answer, x = 3. Both do the same thing to both sides, so the equation stays balanced.
Only BSam divides both sides by 3 first, because 15 divides exactly by 3. Two lines.
The rule
Dividing first is quicker when the number outside the brackets divides the other side exactly. Otherwise, multiplying out first avoids working with fractions early on.
Case C
Solve 3(x + 4) = 20. Which method would you use, and why?
Case C answer
Multiply out first, because 20 does not divide exactly by 3: 3x + 12 = 20, 3x = 8, x = 8/3. Dividing first gives x + 4 = 20/3, and then you have to work out 20/3 − 4.
Probe
Change 3(x + 2) = 15 so that Sam's first step becomes the harder one. What did you change?
Think back
Before today, my first step was always ___.

For the AI. Show two correct methods for the same task, each written as a fictional student's working. The methods differ in the first or main step and reach the same correct answer. Choose a case C where one method is clearly the better choice.

Pair 2

Right next to wrong

A correct and an incorrect solution to the same task, side by side.

What to look for
  • Find the first line where the two solutions differ.
  • Say which rule the wrong step breaks.
  • Check that the correct solution keeps that rule.

Chemistry · Grade 9 · Easier to see

Task: Balance the equation for hydrogen burning in oxygen to make water: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

A · Mia2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Left: 4 H, 2 O. Right: 4 H, 2 O.
B · LeoThis solution has an error.
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂
Left: 2 H, 2 O. Right: 2 H, 2 O.
Only AMia changes the coefficients, the numbers in front of the formulas, and keeps the product as H₂O.
BothBoth start from the same reactants, and both end with the same number of each atom on each side.
Only BLeo changes a subscript, the small number inside the formula, so the product becomes H₂O₂, hydrogen peroxide, not water.
The rule
Balance an equation by changing the coefficients (the numbers in front of the formulas), never the subscripts (the small numbers inside them). Changing a subscript changes the substance.
Case C
Balance the equation for magnesium burning in oxygen: Mg + O₂ → MgO.
Case C answer
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Writing MgO₂ would balance the oxygen but describe a different substance.
Probe
Leo's equation is balanced. So why is it still wrong for this task?
Think back
The difference I nearly missed was ___.

For the AI. Show a correct and an incorrect solution to the same task, each written as a fictional student's working. The incorrect one rests on one common misconception, and its first line is "This solution has an error." Apart from that, keep the two as alike as possible.

Pair 3

One thing changed

Two cases are identical except for one thing.

What to look for
  • Name the one thing that is different.
  • Compare what happened, not just what was done.
  • Ask what the difference in results tells you about that one thing.

Biology · Grade 7 · Harder to see

Task: Two dishes of cress seeds were set up on Monday. Compare them on Friday.

A · Uncovered dishCress seeds on damp cotton wool, uncovered on a windowsill at room temperature. On Friday the seeds have sprouted: short seedlings with green seed leaves.
B · Covered dishCress seeds on damp cotton wool, on the same windowsill, covered by a light-proof box. On Friday the seeds have sprouted: tall, thin seedlings with pale yellow seed leaves.
Only ALight. The seedlings are short with green seed leaves.
BothWater, warmth, the same windowsill and the same seeds. Both dishes have sprouted.
Only BDarkness under the box. The seedlings are tall and thin with pale yellow seed leaves.
The rule
The cress seeds sprouted in both dishes, so cress seeds do not need light to sprout: the seed uses its own stored food. Light matters afterwards, for the green leaves that make new food.
Case C
Dish C is set up like the other two, with damp cotton wool, but it is left in a dark cupboard. Will the seeds sprout, and what will the seedlings look like on Friday?
Case C answer
Yes, they will sprout, because cress seeds do not need light to sprout. Without light the seedlings will be tall, thin and pale, like those in the covered dish.
Probe
The two dishes look very different. Why is "light makes seeds sprout" still the wrong conclusion?
Think back
At first I looked at ___ instead of what both dishes had in common.

For the AI. Describe two cases, such as two experiments, places or situations, that are identical except for one variable, and say what happened in each. Keep everything else word for word the same, including who carried it out. Do not name the changed variable as the point of the task; it belongs in the teacher key.

Pair 4

Two concepts that are easily confused

Two related ideas that students often mix up, shown in the same context.

What to look for
  • List what each one has that the other does not.
  • Find what they share, so you know why they get mixed up.
  • Make a quick test you could use next time.

English · Grade 7 · Easier to see

Task: Two sentences describe the same hot classroom.

A · SimileThe classroom was like an oven.
B · MetaphorThe classroom was an oven.
Only AThe word "like". It says the classroom resembles an oven.
BothBoth compare the classroom to an oven to show how hot it was. Neither is meant literally.
Only BNo comparing word. It says the classroom is an oven.
The rule
A simile compares two different things using "like" or "as". A metaphor says one thing is another. Both are comparisons, not literal statements.
Case C
"The test was a mountain to climb." Simile or metaphor?
Case C answer
A metaphor: there is no "like" or "as", and it says the test is a mountain.
Probe
Which sentence is stronger, and why might a writer still choose the simile?
Think back
The test I will use next time is ___.

For the AI. Show one clear example of each of two concepts students in this grade often confuse, both in the same context, and label each with its concept. Choose examples that differ in the feature that defines each concept.

Pair 5

Two sources on the same event

Two sources describe the same event but differ in when, by whom or why they were written, which changes how far we can trust them.

What to look for
  • Who wrote each source, when and why?
  • Which details agree, and which do not?
  • Which source would you trust for which question?

History · Grade 9 · Harder to see

Task: Two accounts of the first morning of a strike at a cotton mill in 1912. Both were written for this task.

A · Diary, 1912Mary Dent, mill worker, private diary, 4 May 1912: "Went to the gate at six. Perhaps two hundred of us. Mr Hale, the owner, came out and said the mill would close if we did not go in. Nobody moved. Rain all afternoon." (Written for this task.)
B · Memoir, 1950Mary Dent, memoir published in 1950: "I shall never forget the morning a thousand of us stood at the gates in brilliant sunshine, and the owner fled before our courage." (Written for this task.)
Only AWritten the same day, in a private diary. Small, plain details: the time, the rain, about two hundred people.
BothThe same eyewitness and the same event: a crowd at the gate and the owner facing them.
Only BWritten 38 years later, for readers. Bigger numbers, sunshine and a heroic story.
The rule
Even the same eyewitness becomes less reliable on details when writing long after the event for an audience. For details, prefer accounts written close to the event, and be more cautious with any account written to persuade; later accounts show how the event was remembered.
Case C
A union leaflet printed the next day, calling on other workers to join, says "thousands stood firm at Hale's gate". How far would you trust its number?
Case C answer
Not far. It was written close to the event, but to persuade, so the number may be inflated. Check it against Source A.
Probe
What can Source B tell us that Source A cannot?
Think back
I trusted ___ before I checked when and why it was written.

For the AI. Write two short sources about the same event, each with an author, a date and a purpose. Mark both "(written for this task)" unless you quote a real source word for word. The sources differ mainly in one thing that affects how far we can trust them, such as when, by whom or why they were written.
