Barrier Planner

Start from the barrier you see in a task, not from a label. Find it among eight common barriers, read what helps the whole class, then build a prompt that fits those moves to your own task. No diagnosis and no student details, ever.

Our example, not a tested plan.

How it works

  1. Name what you see in the task, in plain words: what happens, not who it happens to.
  2. Find the closest barrier and use the whole-class moves first. They lower the barrier for everyone, and most students need nothing more.
  3. Add one targeted support for the students who still meet it, decide what you will take away first, and set a date to look again.

Eight barriers

Have a diagnosis in mind? Two students with the same diagnosis can need very different things, and many students who need support have none. Describe what you see in this task instead, and pick the closest barrier below.

Describe the task and the barrier, never the student.

Doesn't get started on the task

Framework

What you see

  • Waits after the task is set
  • Asks what to do when the others have started
  • Starts late, or only once you come over

For the whole class

  • Show the first step and do it together, so everyone has started before anyone works alone.
  • Put a worked example on the board. Worked examples help learners with little prior knowledge most (0.51, a moderate effect). Strong
  • Write the steps as a short checklist that students check off. Framework

Targeted

  • A check after the first few minutes with the students who have not started: give them the first step, then move on (our suggestion, not a research finding).

Not this

  • Less work as the default. There is little evidence for it, and it risks lowering expectations.

Look for in three weeks

Do they start within the first few minutes without asking what to do?

Sources: Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025, Learning and Instruction, EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools, Harrison et al. 2013, Review of Educational Research

Pedagogy Compass: They don't get started

Loses track of instructions with several steps

Framework

What you see

  • Does the first step, then stops
  • Asks what comes next
  • Skips steps or does them in the wrong order

For the whole class

  • Number the steps on the board and leave them up for the whole task.
  • Put a checklist of the steps on each table. Framework
  • Show the first step before students start. Splitting instruction into segments gives small-to-medium gains in multimedia learning. Framework

Targeted

  • A quick check after step two with the students who still lose track (our suggestion, not a research finding).

Look for in three weeks

Do they finish the task without asking what comes next?

Sources: Rey et al. 2019, Educational Psychology Review, EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools

Pedagogy Compass: My lessons overload them

Reading a long text takes all their energy

Framework

What you see

  • Reads slowly and runs out of time
  • Can talk about the topic but not about the text
  • Gets lost in long paragraphs

For the whole class

  • Teach the key words before the text, and give a glossary. Glossaries were studied with second-language readers. Promising
  • Add one picture or diagram that shows the main idea. Graphics raised reading comprehension by 0.39, a moderate effect, at all grade levels. Strong
  • Split the text into sections with one guiding question each. Framework

Targeted

  • Text-to-speech or read-aloud for the long text: 0.35 on reading comprehension for students with reading difficulties, between a small and a moderate effect. It gives access to the content but does not teach reading. Promising

Not this

  • Colored overlays and dyslexia fonts. Better-designed studies of overlays show little benefit, and two studies of dyslexia fonts with children found no gain.

Look for in three weeks

Do they answer the section questions without help with the words?

Sources: Guo, Zhang, Wright & McTigue 2020, AERA Open, Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner 2018, Journal of Learning Disabilities, Abraham 2008, Computer Assisted Language Learning, Griffiths, Taylor, Henderson & Barrett 2016, Ophthalmic and Physiological Optics, Kuster, van Weerdenburg, Gompel & Bosman 2018, Annals of Dyslexia

Starts written tasks but doesn't finish them

Framework

What you see

  • Writes a good opening, then stops
  • Runs out of ideas halfway
  • Hands in much less than the task asks for

For the whole class

  • Share the criteria before anyone writes, and show a model text once students have a first draft.
  • Add a short planning step before the writing starts: the main point and what supports it (our suggestion, not a research finding).
  • Offer sentence starters, and take them away as students manage. Frames should be faded, because scaffolding can quietly strip the demand out of a task.

Targeted

  • Let them say the answer to a partner first, then write it (our suggestion, not a research finding).

Not this

  • Less work as the default. Cut only volume that does not serve the goal.

Look for in three weeks

Do they finish the written task without you sitting beside them?

Sources: To, Panadero & Carless 2022, Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education, EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools

Quality Ladder: model texts and criteria

Loses focus during independent work or in noise

Framework

What you see

  • Looks around or chats once independent work starts
  • Is pulled off task by noise or movement
  • Works well with you, drifts when you move on

For the whole class

  • Seat students in rows for individual work. Rows raised on-task behavior during individual work, most for disruptive students, across 8 studies; let the task decide the layout. Promising
  • Teach the routines for independent work explicitly, and practice them.
  • Give a short reminder just before a known trouble spot (precorrection). Promising

Targeted

  • Specific praise when they get it right: say what they did. Promising
  • A quieter seat for independent work (our suggestion; we have found no study of "preferential seating" for ADHD).

Not this

  • Fidget spinners. Attention in class got worse in a study of 60 young children with ADHD.
  • Accommodations written in by habit. A systematic review found very little evidence for the ones commonly recommended for students with behavioral challenges.

Look for in three weeks

Do they keep working through independent work without reminders?

Sources: Wannarka & Ruhl 2008, Support for Learning, Ennis, Royer, Lane & Griffith 2017, Education and Treatment of Children, Royer, Lane, Dunlap & Ennis 2018, Remedial and Special Education, Graziano, Garcia & Landis 2020, Journal of Attention Disorders, Harrison et al. 2013, Review of Educational Research

Finds changes and unstructured time hard

Framework

What you see

  • Gets upset or stuck when the plan changes
  • Struggles at the start of lessons and between activities
  • Drifts in group work without clear roles

For the whole class

  • Put the lesson's plan on the board as a visual schedule, and point to it as you go. Framework
  • Just before each change, say what is ending, what comes next and what to do (precorrection). Promising
  • Teach transitions as routines, and practice them.

Targeted

  • A personal visual schedule. The studies are mostly single-case studies with autistic learners, so plan from what you see, not from a diagnosis. Promising

Look for in three weeks

Do they move to the next activity without stopping to ask what is happening?

Sources: Knight, Sartini & Spriggs 2015, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, Ennis, Royer, Lane & Griffith 2017, Education and Treatment of Children

Can do it with you, but not alone

Framework

What you see

  • Gets it right when you work through it together
  • Stops when you move on
  • Asks you to check each step

For the whole class

  • A worked example, then a half-finished one, then their own. The EEF describes scaffolding as "temporary support" that is removed as students manage. Strong
  • Questions students ask themselves to plan, check and evaluate their work. Metacognition and self-regulation add about 8 months of progress in the EEF Toolkit. Strong

Targeted

  • A partly completed example with the next step left blank.

Look for in three weeks

Do they finish a similar task on their own without asking you to check each step?

Sources: Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025, Learning and Instruction, EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Metacognition and self-regulation, EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools

Pedagogy Compass: They can do it with me, but not alone

Is still learning the language of instruction

Framework

What you see

  • Follows when you show, loses the thread when you only talk
  • Knows the idea but not the words for it
  • Writes much less than they can say

For the whole class

  • Teach a set of academic words intensively across several days. The IES practice guide for elementary and middle school rates the evidence as strong.
  • Let students talk, then write, in content lessons. The IES practice guide rates combining oral and written language as strong too.
  • Give sentence frames, and fade them as students manage.

Targeted

  • An elaborated version of the text and a glossary of the key words. Glossaries had a medium effect on comprehension and a large one on vocabulary, in studies of on-screen glosses for second-language readers. Promising

Not this

  • Stripping the language out of the text. Simplifying removes the language students need to learn; elaborate instead.

Look for in three weeks

Do they use the key words in their own sentences without the glossary?

Sources: Baker et al. 2014, IES practice guide: Teaching academic content and literacy to English learners in elementary and middle school, Abraham 2008, Computer Assisted Language Learning, Oh 2001, TESOL Quarterly

Needs and measures

Framework

Keep each record as a short cycle of assess, plan, do and review, written as needs and measures: what the student meets in school and what will be done, never their difficulties or a diagnosis. Plan one to three measures, each with an owner, a start and what you will look for. Set one review date, and on that date keep, change or stop each measure.

Popular but unsupported

Six adjustments that are often recommended but have little or no support in research, each with what to do instead.

  • Colored overlays and tinted lenses A systematic review found that the better-designed studies show less benefit, with effects small or similar to placebo. Instead: Teach the key words before the text, add a picture, and offer text-to-speech for a long text.
  • Dyslexia fonts Two studies of children with dyslexia found no gain in reading; in the larger one, with 170 children, most preferred Arial. Instead: Keep your usual font, and put the effort into the key words, a glossary and sections with one question each.
  • Fidget spinners as an attention aid Attention in class got worse in a study of 60 young children with ADHD. Instead: Rows for individual work, routines taught explicitly and a reminder before a known trouble spot.
  • Extra time as a classroom support Extra time is a test accommodation. In tests it tended to help all students, somewhat more those with disabilities; it is not a support for learning in lessons. Instead: In lessons, change the route: steps on the board, a worked example and a check after the first step.
  • Less work as the default accommodation There is little evidence for it, and it risks lowering expectations. Instead: Keep the task and the goal, and cut only volume that does not serve the goal.
  • Matching teaching to a diagnosis category The EEF notes that "two children with the same diagnosis can have very different educational needs as individuals". Instead: Start from the barrier you see in the task, and pick the closest card above.

Movement breaks are not on this list: whether they help in class is an open question, resting on lab data about working memory.

What the research says

Planning from the barrier rather than the diagnosis rests on expert guidance. Many of the moves it leads to have good evidence of their own; the chip on each move says how much.

  • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that working this way improves learning.
  • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
  • Framework A useful structure. The method itself has not been tested in intervention studies.
  • Choosewise Our own rule, not a research finding.

Framework Needs, not diagnosis The EEF's guidance on special educational needs says that for most students with special educational needs a diagnosis is "less helpful for teaching and learning than determining the pupils' educational needs". Labels also change what adults expect: in US data on about 11,740 adolescents, teachers and parents expected less of students labeled with a learning disability than of students who achieved and behaved the same without the label (an association, not a measured cause). Elementary school teachers asked about "dyslexia" tied how effective they felt to the belief that the condition is fixed; the wording "reading difficulties" did not have that effect. Sources: EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools, Shifrer 2013, Journal of Health and Social Behavior, Gibbs & Elliott 2015, European Journal of Special Needs Education

Strong Good teaching for all The EEF recommends five strategies for every student: flexible grouping, cognitive and metacognitive strategies, explicit instruction, technology, and scaffolding. In its words, "to a great extent, good teaching for pupils with SEND is good teaching for all". Several of the parts have strong evidence of their own: metacognition and self-regulation add about 8 months of progress in the EEF Toolkit, and worked examples help learners with little prior knowledge most (0.51, a moderate effect). Sources: EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools, EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Metacognition and self-regulation, Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod 2025, Learning and Instruction

Promising Text-to-speech and read-aloud Text-to-speech and related read-aloud tools raised reading comprehension for students with reading difficulties by 0.35, between a small and a moderate effect. They give access to the content; they do not teach reading. Source: Wood, Moxley, Tighe & Wagner 2018, Journal of Learning Disabilities

Promising Visual schedules Visual activity schedules meet the criteria for an evidence-based practice for autistic learners, especially combined with systematic teaching. Most of the 31 studies were single-case studies, from preschool to adult, so plan from what you see, not from a diagnosis. Source: Knight, Sartini & Spriggs 2015, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders

Promising Rows and reminders Rows raised on-task behavior during individual work, most for disruptive students, across 8 studies; let the task decide the layout. A reminder just before a known trouble spot (precorrection) is classed as an evidence-based practice in a review of single-case studies from preschool to Grade 12. Sources: Wannarka & Ruhl 2008, Support for Learning, Ennis, Royer, Lane & Griffith 2017, Education and Treatment of Children

Strong Pictures and diagrams Adding graphics had a moderate effect on reading comprehension, 0.39 across 39 experiments, "regardless of grade level". Pictures, pictorial diagrams and flow diagrams did about equally well. Source: Guo, Zhang, Wright & McTigue 2020, AERA Open

Promising Elaborate rather than simplify, and add a glossary In studies of second-language readers, elaborated texts were understood about as well as simplified ones, or better, and they keep the language students need to learn. We have found no meta-analysis of the comparison. Glossaries had a medium effect on comprehension and a large one on vocabulary, in studies of on-screen glosses. Sources: Yano, Long & Ross 1994, Language Learning, Oh 2001, TESOL Quarterly, Abraham 2008, Computer Assisted Language Learning

Promising The same task, with scaffolds you can take away In elementary school, differentiation gave small to moderate gains when it was part of a broader program or used computers, while grouping students by ability on its own had a small negative effect on the lowest-achieving students. A separate easier worksheet risks lowering the demand for the students who need the content most, so keep the task and add support you can take away. Source: Deunk et al. 2018, Educational Research Review

Framework Assess, plan, do, review The cycle comes from the EEF's guidance. Its evidence review found no systematic review of it in England, and only some positive evidence from US response-to-intervention studies. The needs-and-measures form on this page is ours, built on that guidance. Source: EEF 2020, Special Educational Needs in Mainstream Schools

Watch out for

  • A student becomes identifiable from the details Describe the task and the barrier for "some students". In a very small class, use the card and skip AI.
  • The reply suggests something unsupported or lowers the goal Check it against the card's "Not this" and the list of popular but unsupported adjustments, and keep the same goal for everyone before you use it.
  • Supports that never go away Follow the TAKE IT AWAY part of the reply, and look again on the review date.
  • Errors in definitions and brackets Read every definition and bracketed explanation before you print.
  • The barrier is not the whole story If nothing changes by the review date, talk with colleagues and your school's support staff. Plans for one student belong there, outside AI.
