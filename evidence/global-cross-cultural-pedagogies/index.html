Global & Cross-Cultural Pedagogies 0 interventions

Pedagogical traditions that don't all originate in — or get evaluated by — Western meta-analytic research: Singapore Math's Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract sequence, Marton's Variation Theory (developed in Sweden and Hong Kong), Finnish phenomenon-based learning, Reggio Emilia's documentation practice, Ubuntu collective-knowledge tasks, Indigenous place-based inquiry, and the culturally responsive teaching tradition built on Banks, Gay, Ladson-Billings and Hammond. Editorial note: Western meta-analytic standards apply unevenly to non-Western pedagogical traditions. Variation Theory and CPA carry robust meta-analyses; culturally responsive teaching has a growing meta-analytic base; Ubuntu, Reggio, and place-based pedagogies operate in interpretive scholarly traditions without standardised effect sizes. We show that gap honestly rather than excluding traditions for not being measured on Western terms — the gap is partly a measurement problem, not always a quality problem.

How to read these numbers

  • "Months of progress" is a teacher-friendly shorthand from the EEF Toolkit. It is not directly comparable across studies — different meta-analyses use different baselines, age groups, and outcome measures. Treat it as a magnitude indicator, not a precise prediction.
  • Cohen's d is the standardised effect size used in the original meta-analyses. d ≈ 0.40 is Hattie's "hinge point" — the average effect of a year of schooling. Higher = larger relative effect, but context matters more than the number.
  • Effect sizes are averages. A skill that shows large average effects can still produce small or negative effects in a specific classroom. Use these as a starting point for professional judgment, not a substitute for it.
  • Sources are dated. Where multiple meta-analyses exist, we lead with the most recent quality study and cross-reference EEF where available.
  • Implementation cost (Low / Medium / High) is a practical signal — not an exact science — of what your school needs to invest in teacher time, training, and structural changes to actually run the intervention well. It is the editorial team's reading of what the intervention typically requires in practice. Use it to gauge whether something is straightforward to introduce or a larger undertaking — not as a budget figure.
  • Some interventions are also priced in £ (UK) by the EEF Toolkit. For monetary cost data, see the EEF Teaching & Learning Toolkit.
  • Colour bands signal calibration, not value. An intervention in the "below typical" band is not "bad" — it means the intervention's average effect is below the typical effect of a year of schooling. That can still be appropriate for specific contexts the average does not capture.
  • "No independent meta-analysis found" is not the same as "doesn't work". Several traditions in this domain — Reggio Emilia, Ubuntu pedagogy, place-based and Indigenous education — operate in interpretive scholarly traditions that don't translate cleanly into Cohen's d. The absence of a number is information, not a judgment.

Variation Theory Task Designer

Design tasks built on Marton's Variation Theory — make critical features of a concept discernible by holding everything constant except the feature you want students to see. Four patterns: contrast (varying values to reveal an aspect), separation (varying one aspect while others are fixed), generalization (varying surface appearance while the principle is fixed), fusion (varying multiple critical aspects simultaneously). Anchored on the meta-analysis of the underlying mechanism — comparing cases and contrasting examples — at d=0.50 (Alfieri et al. 2013). The pedagogical theory (Marton, Pang, Kullberg, Runesson) has a substantial Hong Kong and Swedish learning-study tradition built around it, but that tradition reports outcomes in interpretive case-study form rather than pooled effect sizes — so we anchor on the mechanism meta-analysis.

Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract Sequence Designer

Design a Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract learning sequence — start with manipulatives that let students physically model the concept (Cuisenaire rods, base-10 blocks, fraction bars), move to pictorial representations that preserve the structural relationships (bar models, number lines, area diagrams), then connect to abstract symbolic notation. Built on Bruner's enactive-iconic-symbolic theory and operationalised in Singapore Maths via the bar-model method. Anchored on Sokolowski (2018) meta-analysis of representations in elementary mathematics at g=0.53. Two caveats: a more recent meta on multiple-vs-single representations across all mathematical domains (Liu, Ji & Guo 2026, Educational Psychology Review) found a more modest g=0.29; and the EEF's RCT of UK Mathematics Mastery (which used CPA-based Inspire Maths) showed smaller programme-level effects than the Singapore literature suggests. Effects depend heavily on whether the abstract phase actually arrives — manipulatives held too long, or not connected back to symbolic notation, lose the effect.
