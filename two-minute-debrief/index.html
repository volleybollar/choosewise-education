Two-Minute Debrief

For the lessons that didn't go as planned.

You noticed that students didn't learn what you hoped. Answer four questions before the next lesson takes over: goal, evidence, surprise, next. Your AI tool replies with likely causes, one concrete move for the next lesson and a check that shows whether more students understood.

  • No personal data. Counts and patterns only. Student names and individual results never go in.
  • Any AI tool. The prompt works in Copilot, Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude.
  • Before you meet the group again. Run it right after the lesson or at the end of the day.

The teacher writes

Goal
Solve two-step equations such as 3x + 5 = 20.
Evidence
On question 2 of the exit ticket, 11 of 27 divided by 3 first but left the + 5 unchanged. Several wrote x + 5 = 20 ÷ 3.
Surprise

Next
Word problems with two-step equations, Thursday.

The AI replies

Likely causes
1. Students are not secure in how inverse operations work on the whole equation: they divide by 3 first, which is allowed, and forget that the + 5 must then be divided too. If this is the cause, the same error shows up on every equation where they divide first. 2. Too much new at once: the notation and the two-step method arrived in the same lesson, so working memory ran out. If this is the cause, students get one-step equations right but slip as soon as both steps are needed.
Next lesson
Open with one worked example of 3x + 5 = 20, saying each step aloud, then one half-finished example where students do the last step, then two to try on their own (8 min).
Check first
Solve 4x + 3 = 19. Show both steps. Move on to word problems if about 4 in 5 get x = 4 and every step keeps the equation balanced.
One question
What will you do on Thursday with the students who still divide first?

How it works

  1. Notice. A lesson didn't land: students didn't learn or understand what you hoped. Use the debrief on those lessons, not on every lesson.
  2. Four questions (two minutes). Goal, evidence, surprise, next. A feeling is fine as evidence. Numbers from an exit ticket are better.
  3. Change and check. Try the next-lesson move, and open the lesson with the check.

The loop closes itself. The result of the check becomes the evidence in your next debrief for that group, for example "Check: 20 of 27 right, up from 16." That is how you see that the change worked. Over time, the four questions may become a habit for other lessons too.

      Exit tickets

      An exit ticket turns a feeling into numbers. Digital tickets in Microsoft Forms or Google Forms count the answers for you; paper works too.

      One-question check

      When the goal has one right answer, such as a calculation, a date or a definition.

      Exit ticket [One question on today's goal, with one right answer] Example: Solve 4x + 3 = 19.

      Set it up in Microsoft Forms
      1. Go to forms.office.com and choose New Quiz.
      2. Add a Text question for a written answer, or a Choice question if you give options.
      3. In Settings, untick “Record name”.
      4. Share the link or show the QR code at the end of the lesson.
      5. Open the Responses tab to count right and wrong answers.
      Set it up in Google Forms
      1. Go to forms.google.com and start a blank quiz.
      2. Add a Short answer question and set the correct answer in the answer key.
      3. In Settings, set “Collect email addresses” to “Do not collect”.
      4. Send the link, or show it as a QR code.
      5. Open the Responses tab to see how many got it right.

      On paper: a slip with no name line. Sort into two piles, right and not yet, before you leave the room.

      Hinge question

      When you know the mistakes students tend to make. Each wrong option shows one of them.

      Exit ticket [One question] A … B … C … D … Example: Where does most of the mass of a dry log come from? A the soil B water C the air D sunlight

      Set it up in Microsoft Forms
      1. Go to forms.office.com and choose New Quiz, so Forms can mark the right answer.
      2. Add a Choice question and paste the question and options. Tick the correct option.
      3. In Settings, untick “Record name”.
      4. Share the link or show the QR code at the end of the lesson.
      5. Open the Responses tab: it shows how many chose each option.
      Set it up in Google Forms
      1. Go to forms.google.com and start a blank quiz.
      2. Add a Multiple choice question, paste the question and options, and set the answer key.
      3. In Settings, set “Collect email addresses” to “Do not collect”.
      4. Send the link, or show it as a QR code.
      5. Open the Responses tab: the chart shows the counts for each option.

      Without forms: students hold up A, B, C or D on fingers or cards at the same time, and you count.

      Confidence + one question

      When you want to find students who are sure but wrong. They need a different follow-up from those who know they are unsure.

      Exit ticket 1. How sure are you that you can [today's goal]? 1 not sure, 2 a little sure, 3 quite sure, 4 very sure 2. [One check question]

      Set it up in Microsoft Forms
      1. Go to forms.office.com and choose New Quiz.
      2. Add a Rating question from 1 to 4 for how sure students are.
      3. Add a Choice or Text question for the check, and mark the right answer.
      4. In Settings, untick “Record name”.
      5. Open the Responses tab and compare how sure they were with how many got it right.
      Set it up in Google Forms
      1. Go to forms.google.com and start a blank quiz.
      2. Add a Linear scale question from 1 to 4 for how sure students are.
      3. Add the check question and set the answer key.
      4. In Settings, set “Collect email addresses” to “Do not collect”.
      5. Open the Responses tab and compare the two charts.

      On paper: students write the number and the answer on one slip. Make four piles by number, then check the answers in piles 3 and 4 first.

      Explain it in one sentence

      When the goal is understanding rather than recall. Sort the answers into three piles: got it, partly, not yet.

      Exit ticket In one sentence, explain [today's key idea]. Example: In one sentence, explain why we have day and night.

      Set it up in Microsoft Forms
      1. Go to forms.office.com and choose New Form.
      2. Add a Text question and paste the question.
      3. In Settings, untick “Record name”.
      4. Share the link or show the QR code at the end of the lesson.
      5. Open the Responses tab, read the answers and count got it, partly and not yet.
      Set it up in Google Forms
      1. Go to forms.google.com and start a blank form.
      2. Add a Paragraph question and paste the question.
      3. In Settings, set “Collect email addresses” to “Do not collect”.
      4. Send the link, or show it as a QR code.
      5. Open the Responses tab, read the answers and count got it, partly and not yet.

      On paper: a slip with no name line. Three piles on your desk while students pack up.

      Day and night · 6C science, Grade 6

      6C science · Grade 6

      The teacher writes

      Goal
      Explain why we have day and night: the Earth turns on its axis.
      Evidence
      Only a feeling. When I asked, many said the Sun goes round the Earth, and the nods looked unsure.
      Surprise
      The globe-and-torch demonstration took longer than planned, so there was no time for students to explain it back.
      Next
      The seasons, Monday.

      The AI replies

      Likely causes
      1. A common misconception: everyday experience says the Sun moves across the sky, and one demonstration is not enough to replace that idea. If this is the cause, students say “the Sun moves” even right after the demonstration. 2. Students watched but never had to explain: the demonstration ran out of time, so nobody put the idea into their own words. If this is the cause, students can repeat “the Earth spins” but cannot use it to explain why it is night in another country.
      Next lesson
      Before the seasons, pairs use a torch and a ball to show day and night to each other, and each pair writes one sentence that starts “It is night when…” (7 min).
      Check first
      On your mini whiteboard: When it is day here, it is night in Australia. Why? Move on if about 4 in 5 mention the Earth turning, not the Sun moving. To turn your feeling into evidence, count how many boards say the Sun goes away.
      One question
      Which part of the seasons lesson depends on students already picturing the Earth turning?
      Explaining the effect of a quotation · 10A English, Grade 10

      10A English · Grade 10

      The teacher writes

      Goal
      Explain how the writer creates tension in the opening of the short story, using one quotation.
      Evidence
      One-sentence exit ticket: 9 of 26 explained the effect of a quotation, 12 only retold what happens, and 5 named a technique without saying what it does.
      Surprise
      Students who talked well in pairs wrote much weaker sentences.
      Next
      Paragraph writing on the same story, Wednesday.

      The AI replies

      Likely causes
      1. Students do not yet know what “explain the effect” looks like on paper: retelling and naming a technique feel like analysis to them. If this is the cause, a model sentence will change the answers quickly. 2. The talk did not carry over into writing: in pairs they analysed, but nobody turned the talk into a written sentence before the exit ticket. If this is the cause, students can say the effect aloud but still write a retelling.
      Next lesson
      Show three sentences about the same quotation: one that retells, one that names a technique and one that explains the effect. Students rank them and say why, then rewrite their own exit ticket sentence (10 min).
      Check first
      “The door creaked open, slowly.” In one sentence, explain how this line builds tension. Move on to paragraphs if about 4 in 5 say what the reader feels or expects, not only what happens.
      One question
      What do your strongest pair talkers do differently when they write?
      A follow-up: the Treaty of Versailles · 11 History, Grade 11

      11 History · Grade 11

      The teacher writes

      Goal
      Explain why the Treaty of Versailles caused resentment in Germany, using two causes.
      Evidence
      Check: 19 of 25 gave two causes, up from 11 last lesson. Most still describe the terms rather than explain why they caused resentment.
      Surprise
      The six who missed the check had all been absent from the first lesson on the Treaty.
      Next
      Source analysis on the Treaty, Friday.

      The AI replies

      Likely causes
      1. Students know the terms but not the link to resentment: they list war guilt, reparations and lost land without saying why Germans felt humiliated. If this is the cause, answers improve once students practise adding “because” after each term. 2. Missing prior knowledge for the six who were absent: they never met the terms, so they cannot explain them. If this is the cause, those six will also struggle with the sources on Friday.
      Next lesson
      Give everyone a “term, because, so” frame for two terms, and give the six who were absent a short summary card of the terms to read first (8 min).
      Check first
      Complete the sentence: The war guilt clause caused resentment in Germany because … so … Move on to the sources if about 4 in 5 explain why, not only what.
      One question
      How will you know on Friday whether the six who were absent have caught up?

      Why it works

      • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that teaching this way improves learning.
      • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
      • Framework A useful structure for planning, with no intervention studies behind it as a method.

      Formative assessment: acting on what students show Strong

      Black & Wiliam's 1998 review made the case that formative assessment raises achievement, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 118 K-12 studies confirms a positive, more modest average effect (Yao et al. 2024). A whole-school EEF trial found a similar, real effect at scale.

      Source: Black & Wiliam 1998, Assessment in Education, Yao et al. 2024, Educational Research and Evaluation, EEF: Embedding Formative Assessment evaluation

      Feedback that moves learning Strong

      Feedback is among the best-evidenced approaches in the EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit. A 2020 meta-analysis finds a medium average effect with large variation: feedback that carries rich information about the task and how to improve works much better than praise, rewards or punishment (Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020).

      Source: EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Feedback, Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers in Psychology

      Worked examples and cognitive load Strong

      Cognitive load theory rests on decades of replicated experiments. The worked-example effect is one of its most robust findings, confirmed in a 2023 meta-analysis, and it fades as students gain expertise (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; Barbieri et al. 2023).

      Source: Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019, Educational Psychology Review, Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review

      Hinge questions Framework

      A hinge question is one multiple-choice question at a key point in the lesson, where each wrong option shows a known misconception, so the teacher can decide in a minute whether to move on. It builds on the all-student responses Wiliam (2011) describes, such as letter cards everyone holds up at once. Lab studies with university students show that multiple-choice items with plausible wrong options make students retrieve as well as open questions do (Little et al. 2012). The hinge question itself has had little direct testing in classrooms.

      Source: Wiliam 2011, Studies in Educational Evaluation, Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello 2012, Psychological Science

      Structured teacher reflection Framework

      Reflecting on a lesson in a set structure is widely recommended in teacher education, but no meta-analysis shows that it improves student learning as a method on its own. What makes a difference is what reflection leads to: teachers acting on evidence of what their students learned (Timperley et al. 2007). That is why every debrief starts from the goal and the evidence.

      Source: Timperley, Wilson, Barrar & Fung 2007, Teacher Professional Learning and Development (Best Evidence Synthesis)

      For research on many other teaching strategies, see the Evidence Toolkit on this site.

      AI tools and age rules

      You use AI to think through your lesson. Your students do not need to. If students use AI themselves, these rules apply (October 2026):

      • Copilot Chat: Students aged 13 and over, through the school's own Microsoft 365 account. The school's administrator must set each student's age group and consent.
      • Gemini for Education: Open to education users of all ages, with a stricter experience for under-18s. The school's administrator must turn it on.
      • ChatGPT: Ages 13 and over on personal accounts, with a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. ChatGPT for Teachers is for school staff only.
      • Claude: Ages 18 and over. In a school, for teachers only.

      Rules differ between countries and change often. Check your own national rules and your school policy before students use any AI tool.
