Meetings about teaching

Promising meetings about one problem and pairing on a skill Framework the protocol itself

Give each meeting about teaching one problem in your teaching, and end it with a decision that each of you tries in class. Send information in writing instead, and keep the meeting for teaching.

Meeting protocol

One problem per meeting, a decision that each of you tries in class, and a follow-up at the next meeting.

The problem

One problem many of us see in class, in one sentence.

What each of us brings

A task, a test item or a count from an exit ticket. Student work stays on paper in the room, with names removed, and never goes into an AI tool.

What we saw

What the things we brought show about the problem.

The decision

The one change each of us tries in class.

Who, by when

Each of us, and the date we try the change by.

Follow-up

Date: [date of the next meeting]

What we will look at: [what we will bring to check the change]

Prompts to make your own

These prompts are writing aids, not evidence-based methods.

Do not include student names or student work. Fill in the brackets afterwards, outside AI.

P1 · An agenda for a meeting about one problem

Our team of [number] teachers of [subject or year group] has [length] minutes to work on one problem in our teaching: [the problem]. Write an agenda with times: what each of us brings (a task, a test item or a count from an exit ticket), how we look at it together, the one change we will each try in class, and when and how we check the result at the next meeting. Do not ask me about individual students or colleagues.

Copied

P2 · An information note instead of a meeting

I want to send my team a written note instead of holding a meeting that only passes on information. Here are the points: [list the points]. Turn them into a note under 200 words in plain language, with four parts: who it is for, what is new, what they need to do and by when, and who to ask. If a point needs discussion, do not explain it in the note; list it at the end as a question for our next meeting. Do not ask me for names or details about students or colleagues.

Messages home

Promising short, regular messages about learning

Short, regular messages about one specific thing: work that is missing, a test that is coming, something done well. Agree with your team how many messages a class gets per term, and stay within that limit (our rule, not a research finding).

What the research says

Meetings about one problem and short messages home have promising evidence. Cutting administration rests on expert guidance, not trials. We have found no controlled study of AI for report writing or parent communication by school teachers.

  • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that working this way improves learning.
  • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
  • Framework A useful structure. The method itself has not been tested in intervention studies.
  • Choosewise Our own rule, not a research finding.

Promising Meetings about one problem, quality over hours In a study of more than 9,000 teachers in 336 Miami-Dade schools, it was better quality of collaboration that went with larger gains in maths and reading (Ronfeldt et al. 2015). It is a correlation, not a measured cause. The meeting protocol itself is a Framework: we have found no trial of a meeting protocol on its own. Source: Ronfeldt, Farmer, McQueen & Grissom 2015, American Educational Research Journal

Promising Pairing on a skill In a randomised trial, pairing teachers who scored low on specific skills with colleagues strong in those skills raised student achievement in the low-scoring teachers' classes by 0.12 standard deviations, a small effect (Papay et al. 2020). That is smaller than coaching (0.18 on achievement across 60 causal studies; Kraft et al. 2018), but it came from a colleague rather than a programme. It is a single trial. Sources: Papay, Taylor, Tyler & Laski 2020, American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, Kraft, Blazar & Hogan 2018, Review of Educational Research

Content matters more than format The EEF's trial of lesson study in 181 English primary schools found that pupils did no better on national tests a year after the two-year programme (Murphy et al. 2021). A US trial with 39 teams found larger gains in fractions knowledge when lesson study came with a research-based resource kit (Lewis & Perry 2017). Our reading: strong content, such as research-based materials, matters more than the format of the meeting. Sources: Murphy, Weinhardt & Wyness 2021, Economics of Education Review, Lewis & Perry 2017, Journal for Research in Mathematics Education

Framework Cut first A workload group set up by England's Department for Education saw "no reason" for more than two or three attainment data collection points a year, each used to inform clear actions, and called lengthy written reports to parents "usually burdensome" (DfE 2018). The DfE's marking review lists deep marking and writing "VF" to show that verbal feedback was given as ineffective practice, and says: "If the hours spent do not have the commensurate impact on pupil progress: stop it." (DfE 2016). The EEF's feedback guidance says a policy "should not over-specify features such as the frequency or method of feedback" (EEF 2021). These are examples from England. The guidance comes from expert groups, not from trials, and its value lies in cutting tasks, not in automating them. Sources: Department for Education 2018, Making data work (Teacher Workload Advisory Group), Department for Education 2016, Eliminating unnecessary workload around marking, Education Endowment Foundation 2021, Teacher Feedback to Improve Pupil Learning

Promising Messages about learning In US middle and high schools, weekly automated alerts about missed assignments, grades and absences cut course failures by 27% and raised class attendance by 12%, but did not change state test scores (Bergman & Chan 2021). In a high-school credit-recovery programme, a weekly one-sentence message from the teacher cut the share who failed to earn credit from 15.8% to 9.3% (Kraft & Rogers 2015). In a UK trial, 30 texts over a year about tests, homework and topics gave secondary students about one month's extra progress in maths, a small gain (via the EEF's guidance). The EEF says there is, as yet, less rigorous evidence for what makes parental engagement effective, and that many of the more rigorous studies show mixed results; the impacts of well-designed messages may appear small, but they are generally low cost. Sources: Bergman & Chan 2021, Journal of Human Resources, Kraft & Rogers 2015, Economics of Education Review, Education Endowment Foundation 2018, Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning

Promising Short absence letters In 203 US schools, letters to parents of children who missed more school than average, giving the child's total days absent, cut absences by about one day per child over the year; parents got about four letters each, and the EEF notes a letter can be fewer than 50 words and should not blame (Rogers & Feller 2018, as reported in the EEF's guidance). It is a single trial. The absence message in this pack follows that pattern. Sources: Rogers & Feller 2018, Nature Human Behaviour, Education Endowment Foundation 2018, Working with Parents to Support Children's Learning

AI for these tasks We have found no controlled study of AI for report writing or parent communication by school teachers. Checking is work too: interviews with 57 teachers in Sweden and Australia, reported by the OECD, describe the hidden labour of reviewing and repairing AI drafts for administrative tasks (Selwyn, Ljungqvist & Sonesson 2025). That is why AI writes the template here, once: a template is checked once and reused, and no student's details go into AI. You add them afterwards, outside AI. Sources: OECD 2026, Digital Education Outlook 2026, Selwyn, Ljungqvist & Sonesson 2025, Learning, Media and Technology

Watch out for

  • Wrong details in the wrong message. Fill in the brackets one message at a time, outside AI, and read each message before you send it.
  • Templates that grow. Keep every template to one page. If a template gets longer than what it replaced, cut it.
  • Too many messages. Count what already goes out to each class, and stay within the limit your team agreed.
  • Translation errors. Translate the template before any details go in, keep the brackets unchanged, and where you can, ask someone who reads the language to check it once.
  • A meeting with five problems. Give each meeting one problem, and spread the rest over later meetings.
