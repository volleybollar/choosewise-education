    Copied

    The model map

    About fifty models in six categories. The categories answer different questions: does it work in classrooms, does it help you plan, how does it explain learning, what kind of school is it, and should teachers stop believing it.

    • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that teaching this way improves learning.
    • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
    • Framework A useful structure for planning, with no intervention studies behind it as a method.
    • Theory A way of explaining how learning happens. It shapes values and choices, but cannot be tested as a single intervention.
    • School model A whole-school movement with its own philosophy and practice.
    • Contested The research does not support using it as a teaching method, even though many teachers believe in it.

    A Strong Strong

    Does it work in classrooms? Yes: replicated, backed by meta-analyses.

    Retrieval practice StrongStudents pull what they have learned out of memory, instead of reading it again.

    The Learning Scientists; Roediger & Karpicke

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    The lesson opens with five quick questions answered from memory, no notes. Students check their answers afterwards, and the teacher notes which question most of the class missed. Homework is three closed-book questions instead of re-reading notes.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft a bank of short recall questions from your unit plan in minutes, including questions on content from weeks ago, so you only need to check and pick.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students ask a chatbot for a summary to re-read, or look up the answer before trying, the recall step disappears and so does most of the benefit.
    The evidence
    Practice testing is one of two strategies rated high utility in the best-known review of study techniques (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Meta-analyses of classroom studies confirm it improves learning in school (Yang et al. 2021; Ruitenburg et al. 2026).

    Source: Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Yang et al. 2021, Psychological Bulletin, Ruitenburg et al. 2026, Educational Psychology Review

    Spaced practice StrongPractice is spread out over days and weeks, so earlier content keeps coming back.

    The Learning Scientists; Cepeda et al.

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Instead of 25 questions on today's topic, students do 12 today and the rest as short starters over the next three weeks. Every starter mixes in one or two items from earlier in the term.
    Where AI helps
    AI can turn your unit plan into a simple schedule of short review sets, showing which earlier topics come back in which lesson.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI makes it easy to produce a fresh worksheet on today's topic every lesson, which pulls practice back into one big block instead of spreading it out.
    The evidence
    Distributed practice is the other strategy rated high utility by Dunlosky et al. 2013. A 2026 meta-analysis of studies in real school classrooms finds a small but reliable benefit from spacing practice out (Ruitenburg et al. 2026), and a 2022 review finds that spacing and retrieval together give the most durable learning (Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022).

    Source: Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Ruitenburg et al. 2026, Educational Psychology Review, Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022, Nature Reviews Psychology

    Cognitive load and worked examples StrongNew material is shown in small steps with worked examples, so working memory is not overloaded.

    Sweller; Kalyuga

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    The teacher introduces one new idea at a time and shows two fully worked examples. Students then finish a half-done example before they start on their own, beginning with the easiest problems. As students get better, the support is taken away.
    Where AI helps
    AI can quickly produce worked examples and half-finished versions of the same problem, so you can build a fading sequence without writing every step yourself.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI tools often answer with long, dense text and many new terms at once, which overloads novices. Students who get full solutions on demand also skip the step where they do the work themselves.
    The evidence
    Cognitive load theory rests on decades of replicated experiments. The worked-example effect is one of its most robust findings, confirmed in a 2023 meta-analysis, and it fades as students gain expertise (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; Barbieri et al. 2023).

    Source: Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019, Educational Psychology Review, Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review

    Explicit instruction StrongThe teacher models, guides practice and checks understanding before students work on their own.

    Stockard et al.; Rosenshine

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    The teacher thinks aloud through two examples, then the class does three together on mini whiteboards. Students move to independent work only when most of the class gets them right.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft a think-aloud script for a hard step, or a set of guided-practice questions that rise in difficulty, which you then adjust to your class.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students turn to AI for explanations during independent work, you lose the check that tells you whether they were ready to work alone.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis of 328 studies over half a century found consistently positive effects of Direct Instruction, a highly structured form of explicit teaching (Stockard et al. 2018). Rosenshine's principles summarize the wider research on explicit instruction for classroom use.

    Source: Stockard et al. 2018, Review of Educational Research, Rosenshine 2012, American Educator

    Feedback StrongFeedback tells students where they are going, how they are doing and what to do next, and they act on it.

    Hattie & Timperley

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Marked essays come back without the grade at first. Students get 15 minutes to rewrite one paragraph using the comment on it. The teacher picks the most common weakness across the class and models one fix.
    Where AI helps
    AI can sort a stack of student work by common weaknesses, so you can give whole-class feedback on the one or two things most students need next.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI can write long, polished comments for every student, which adds text but not time to act on it. If students let AI fix their work, the feedback improves the text, not the student.
    The evidence
    Feedback is among the best-evidenced approaches in the EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit. A 2020 meta-analysis finds a medium average effect with large variation: feedback that carries rich information about the task and how to improve works much better than praise, rewards or punishment (Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020).

    Source: EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Feedback, Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers in Psychology

    Metacognition and self-regulation StrongStudents plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning, and the teacher shows them how.

    EEF guidance report

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Before a task, the teacher models out loud how to plan it and what to do when stuck. Students write one line on how they will check their own work, and use it before they hand in.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft planning and self-check prompts tied to the exact task, so reflection is about this piece of work and not a generic form.
    Where AI gets in the way
    When AI plans the task, tracks progress and checks the result, students never practice doing those things themselves.
    The evidence
    Metacognition and self-regulation is one of the highest-impact, best-evidenced approaches in the EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit (+8 months, 4 padlocks), when it is taught explicitly inside subject content.

    Source: EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Metacognition and self-regulation

    Mastery learning StrongContent is split into units, and students show they have mastered one before moving to the next, with extra help if needed.

    Bloom; Guskey

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    At the end of a unit, students take a short check. Those who reach the bar get an extension task, and those who do not get targeted re-teaching and a second check. The class still moves on together.
    Where AI helps
    AI can produce parallel versions of a unit check and short re-teaching tasks for each common gap, so a second attempt is not the same test again.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI-driven platforms can let every student move at their own pace with no teacher in the loop, which is the version of mastery learning that tends to work less well.
    The evidence
    Mastery learning has a long research record: a meta-analysis of mastery programs found positive effects on achievement (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990). The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit rates it at about +5 months, and notes it works less well when students work entirely at their own pace.

    Source: EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Mastery learning, Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, Review of Educational Research

    Collaborative learning StrongStudents learn together in small groups on a structured task, where everyone has a part and is accountable for it.

    Johnson & Johnson

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Groups of three get a problem with clear roles, and every student must be able to explain the group's answer. The teacher calls on any member at random, so no one can coast.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you design group tasks with distinct roles and a shared goal, and draft prompts that make each student's contribution visible.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If one student asks AI for the answer, the group stops thinking together. The discussion the task was built around never happens.
    The evidence
    The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit rates collaborative learning at about +5 months, with structured tasks and individual accountability working best. A meta-analysis of face-to-face cooperative learning confirms positive effects on achievement (Kyndt et al. 2013).

    Source: EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Collaborative learning approaches, Kyndt et al. 2013, Educational Research Review

    Reciprocal teaching StrongStudents take turns leading a small group through a text: predicting, questioning, clarifying and summarizing.

    Palincsar & Brown

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    The teacher first models the four strategies on a short text. Then groups of four read the next section, and each student leads one strategy before the roles rotate.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you split a text into sections and draft model questions and summaries to show students what a good one looks like.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students ask AI to summarize the text or explain the hard parts, they skip the questioning and clarifying that the method is built to train.
    The evidence
    Reciprocal teaching was developed and tested by Palincsar & Brown (1984). A review of later studies found consistent gains in reading comprehension, larger on researcher-made tests than on standardized ones (Rosenshine & Meister 1994). It belongs to the reading comprehension strategies the EEF Toolkit rates among its strongest approaches.

    Source: Palincsar & Brown 1984, Cognition and Instruction, Rosenshine & Meister 1994, Review of Educational Research, EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Reading comprehension strategies

    Formative assessment StrongThe teacher checks what students understood during the lesson and adjusts the next step based on what they show.

    Black & Wiliam; Wiliam

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Before moving on, the teacher asks one multiple-choice hinge question and everyone shows a letter at once. If too many choose a wrong option, the teacher re-teaches before the worksheet. An exit ticket decides how tomorrow starts.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft hinge questions where each wrong option matches a common misconception, and sort exit tickets into secure, partly and not yet.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students use AI to answer checks or homework, what you see is the tool's understanding, not theirs, and your next step is built on the wrong evidence.
    The evidence
    Black & Wiliam's 1998 review made the case that formative assessment raises achievement, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 118 K-12 studies confirms a positive, more modest average effect (Yao et al. 2024). A whole-school EEF trial found a similar, real effect at scale.

    Source: Black & Wiliam 1998, Assessment in Education, Yao et al. 2024, Educational Research and Evaluation, EEF: Embedding Formative Assessment evaluation

    B Promising Promising

    Does it work in classrooms? Supported, but mixed, short-term or dependent on context.

    Interleaving PromisingDifferent types of problems are mixed in practice, so students must decide which method fits each one.

    Rohrer; Brunmair & Richter

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    After students have learned area of triangles, circles and rectangles separately, the practice set mixes all three. Students first name the shape and the formula, then solve.
    Where AI helps
    AI can turn three blocked worksheets into one mixed set and add a 'which method?' step before each problem.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students ask AI which method to use, they skip the exact choice that interleaving is meant to train.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis finds a moderate overall benefit from mixing categories during practice. The benefit is strongest for paintings and other visual material, small for maths and unclear for texts, and blocking worked better for learning words (Brunmair & Richter 2019). A 2026 meta-analysis of school classroom studies finds a similar average benefit (Ruitenburg et al. 2026).

    Source: Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin, Ruitenburg et al. 2026, Educational Psychology Review

    Elaboration PromisingStudents explain why and how things work, and connect new ideas to what they already know.

    Dunlosky et al.

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    After reading about the causes of a revolution, students answer 'Why would that lead to unrest?' and 'How is this like the last case we studied?' in pairs. The teacher picks a few answers to sharpen in front of the class.
    Where AI helps
    AI can generate good why- and how-questions for a text or topic, which you check for accuracy and pick from.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI writes the explanation, students read a good answer but never build the connections themselves.
    The evidence
    Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation were rated moderate utility by Dunlosky et al. 2013, mainly because the evidence comes from short studies. A meta-analysis finds that prompting students to explain to themselves improves learning (Bisra et al. 2018), though most studies are short and many involve university students.

    Source: Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Bisra et al. 2018, Educational Psychology Review

    Concrete examples PromisingAbstract ideas are taught through several concrete examples, so students see the principle behind them.

    The Learning Scientists

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    To teach opportunity cost, the teacher gives three examples from different settings and asks what they have in common. Students then judge a new case: is this opportunity cost or not?
    Where AI helps
    AI can generate many varied examples and near-misses for a concept quickly, so you can choose the ones that best show the shared idea.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI examples can be wrong, generic or all from the same setting, which teaches the surface instead of the principle. Check them before use.
    The evidence
    Experiments show that several varied examples help students learn abstract concepts, especially when they compare them (Rawson, Thomas & Jacoby 2015). The Learning Scientists include concrete examples among six strategies from cognitive science (Weinstein, Madan & Sumeracki 2018), but classroom evidence is still limited.

    Source: Rawson, Thomas & Jacoby 2015, Educational Psychology Review, Weinstein, Madan & Sumeracki 2018, Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications

    Dual coding and multimedia principles PromisingWords and a clear visual explain the same idea together, without extra decoration.

    Paivio; Mayer

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Instead of six bullet points read aloud, the teacher draws and labels one diagram live while students label their own copy. The slide shows only the diagram, so students listen and look rather than read and listen.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest which kind of visual fits a concept (timeline, flow chart, labeled diagram) and draft a simple version you redraw.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI makes it easy to fill slides with decorative images that look good but add load without explaining anything.
    The evidence
    An overview of meta-analyses on multimedia design finds reliable benefits from pairing words with relevant visuals and removing extras, mostly in short studies (Noetel et al. 2022). Long-term classroom evidence is thinner.

    Source: Noetel et al. 2022, Review of Educational Research

    Flipped classroom PromisingStudents meet new content before class, often by video, and lesson time goes to practice and discussion.

    Bergmann & Sams

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Students watch a short video at home and answer three quiz questions on it. The lesson starts from the quiz results and spends the time on problems with the teacher close by.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help turn the pre-class video into a short quiz, so you know who prepared and what they missed before the lesson starts.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students let AI summarize the video or answer the quiz, they arrive unprepared, and the lesson built on that preparation falls apart.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis finds a small benefit for learning (g = 0.35), dropping to 0.24 in studies with enough power, and most studies are from higher education (Låg & Sæle 2019). Testing whether students prepared seems to help slightly.

    Source: Låg & Sæle 2019, AERA Open

    Growth mindset PromisingThe belief that ability can grow with effort, good strategies and help from others.

    Dweck

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Praise the strategy and the next step, not effort alone: 'Checking your units caught that error.' Treat mistakes as information, and give students real chances to improve and resubmit.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you rewrite generic praise into feedback that names the strategy that worked and the next step to take.
    Where AI gets in the way
    When AI smooths over every struggle, students rarely get the experience of improving through their own effort, which is what the belief rests on.
    The evidence
    Two meta-analyses found a weak link between mindset and achievement and very small effects of mindset interventions (Sisk et al. 2018). A national study of about 12,000 ninth-graders found a short online intervention raised grades for lower-achieving students by 0.10 grade points, mainly in schools where peer norms supported taking on challenges (Yeager et al. 2019).

    Source: Sisk et al. 2018, Psychological Science, Yeager et al. 2019, Nature

    Productive failure PromisingStudents try a well-designed problem before being taught the method, then the teacher builds on their attempts.

    Kapur

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Before teaching standard deviation, students get data from two teams and try to invent a way to say which is more consistent. The teacher then compares their methods and shows the formal one.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you design a problem with several reasonable solution paths and list the attempts students are likely to make, so you can plan how to build on them.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students ask AI as soon as they get stuck, the struggle that prepares them for the explanation never happens.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis of 53 studies found a moderate benefit (g = 0.36) of problem solving before instruction over the usual order, and larger effects when the design was followed faithfully. Results favored instruction first for younger students (Grades 2–5) and for domain-general skills (Sinha & Kapur 2021). The approach is still debated by researchers who favor explicit instruction.

    Source: Sinha & Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research

    Dialogic teaching PromisingStudents build on each other's ideas in discussion, and the teacher's questions push for reasons.

    Alexander

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    The teacher asks an open question, gives a minute of silent thinking and a minute in pairs, then chooses speakers. The next speaker starts with 'I agree with … because' or 'I see it differently from … because'.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft open questions with more than one reasonable answer, and follow-up moves that push students to give reasons.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students bring AI-written arguments to the discussion, they repeat ready-made points instead of reasoning together.
    The evidence
    An EEF trial of Dialogic Teaching found positive effects in English, science and maths, though a follow-up found no effect on later national tests. A large classroom study links participation and elaborated reasoning in whole-class talk to better outcomes (Howe et al. 2019). Results vary with how well it is done.

    Source: EEF: Dialogic Teaching evaluation, Howe et al. 2019, Journal of the Learning Sciences

    Universal Design for Learning PromisingLessons are planned from the start with more than one way to engage, take in content and show learning.

    CAST

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the core idea: remove barriers that have nothing to do with the goal. Give the text with audio as an option, a glossary on the board, and let students show the same goal in writing, a diagram or orally.
    Where AI helps
    AI can quickly make an easier-to-read version, an audio version or a glossary of a text, so access does not depend on your evening hours.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI makes it easy to produce many versions of everything, which spreads your time thin and can lower the demand for some students instead of removing barriers.
    The evidence
    Studies of UDL report mostly positive results, but they apply and describe UDL in very different ways, which makes them hard to compare (Rao, Ok & Bryant 2014). A 2024 review of the studies CAST cites for its guidelines found little direct evidence for them (Boysen 2024).

    Source: Rao, Ok & Bryant 2014, Remedial and Special Education, Boysen 2024, Policy Futures in Education

    Generative learning PromisingStudents learn by producing something from the content: a summary, a map, a drawing or an explanation to others.

    Fiorella & Mayer

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    After reading a text on the immune system, students draw the steps as a labeled flow chart from memory, then compare with a partner and fix what is missing.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest which generative task fits a text (map, drawing, summary, explanation) and draft a model to show after students have tried.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI produces the summary, map or explanation, the student gets the product without the thinking that makes it work.
    The evidence
    Fiorella & Mayer (2016) describe eight strategies where students make sense of material by producing something. A review of six popular ones finds that all six work for university students, but evidence for younger students is mixed: some, like drawing and questioning, seem to work only from secondary school (Brod 2021).

    Source: Fiorella & Mayer 2016, Educational Psychology Review, Brod 2021, Educational Psychology Review

    C Framework Framework

    Does it help you think and plan? Useful, but not testable as an intervention.

    Prior knowledge and advance organisers FrameworkNew learning is built on what students already know, so find out what that is before you build on it.

    Ausubel

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Open a new topic with three quick questions on what the unit depends on, answered from memory. Before new content, give a short overview that links it to something students already know. Fix the gaps you find before the first real task.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft a short diagnostic quiz on the knowledge a unit depends on, with wrong options built on common misconceptions, for you to check and trim.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students look up the answers to a prior-knowledge check, the check shows what the tool knows, not what they know, and the gaps stay hidden.
    The evidence
    What students know before a unit strongly predicts what they know after it. But a meta-analysis of 8,776 effect sizes found that, on average, prior knowledge does not predict how much students gain, and that this varies widely between studies (Simonsmeier et al. 2022). An early meta-analysis found small positive effects of advance organisers (Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980); checking prior knowledge early is a planning step, not a tested method.

    Source: Simonsmeier, Flaig, Deiglmayr, Schalk & Schneider 2022, Educational Psychologist, Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980, American Educational Research Journal

    Bloom's taxonomy FrameworkSorts goals and questions by kind of thinking: remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create.

    Bloom; Anderson & Krathwohl

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Look at your questions for a lesson and check which levels they ask for. If every question is recall, add one that asks students to apply or judge. Do not skip remembering: students cannot analyse what they do not know.
    Where AI helps
    AI can turn one topic into a set of questions at different levels, which you then pick from and sharpen for your class.
    Where AI gets in the way
    A task labelled "create" or "evaluate" is easy to hand to AI, so students can produce higher-level work without doing the thinking the label promises.
    The evidence
    Bloom's revised taxonomy (Krathwohl 2002) is widely used to plan and judge the depth of questions and tasks. It is a language for planning, not a method tested in intervention studies.

    Source: Krathwohl 2002, Theory Into Practice

    SOLO and constructive alignment FrameworkSOLO describes how deep an answer goes, from one idea to linked and extended ideas. Alignment makes goals, tasks and tests match.

    Biggs

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Before a task, show students what a one-idea answer, a several-ideas answer and a linked answer look like on the same question. Then check that the task and the test ask for the same depth as your goal.
    Where AI helps
    AI can write sample answers at each SOLO level on your question, which students sort and discuss before they write their own.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI text often looks linked and extended on the surface, so students and teachers can mistake fluent writing for deep understanding.
    The evidence
    The SOLO taxonomy (Biggs & Collis 1982) is widely used to plan and judge the depth of tasks. Constructive alignment makes the point that goals, activities and assessment should match (Biggs 1996). Both are frameworks for planning, not methods tested in intervention studies.

    Source: Biggs & Collis 1982, Evaluating the Quality of Learning: The SOLO Taxonomy, Biggs 1996, Higher Education

    Backward design FrameworkPlan a unit from the end: what students should be able to do, how that will show, and only then the activities.

    Wiggins & McTighe

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Before choosing activities, write down what students should be able to do by the end and what task will show it. Then cut any activity that does not lead there.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft an end-of-unit task and a short list of evidence from your goal, so you start planning from the target instead of the textbook.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If the final task is one AI can complete, the evidence you planned for stops showing what students can do, and the whole design loses its anchor.
    The evidence
    Backward design starts from what students should be able to do and how that will show, and plans the activities last (Wiggins & McTighe 2005). Constructive alignment makes the same point: goals, activities and assessment should match (Biggs 1996). Both are frameworks for planning, not methods tested in intervention studies.

    Source: Wiggins & McTighe 2005, Understanding by Design (ASCD), Biggs 1996, Higher Education

    Gradual release of responsibility FrameworkResponsibility moves step by step from teacher to student: I do, we do, you do.

    Pearson & Gallagher

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Model one example while thinking aloud, solve the next one together, then let pairs try one before students work alone. Move on when most succeed, not when the clock says so.
    Where AI helps
    AI can produce a series of examples that go from fully worked to half-finished to blank, so the release of support is built into the material.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students can ask AI at the "you do" stage, they never really take over the work, and the last step of the release does not happen.
    The evidence
    Pearson & Gallagher (1983) described the gradual release of responsibility in reading comprehension. It is a framework, not a tested package. Its steps line up with strong evidence on worked examples that fade as students gain expertise (Barbieri et al. 2023).

    Source: Pearson & Gallagher 1983, Contemporary Educational Psychology, Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review

    Rosenshine's principles of instruction FrameworkTen principles of effective teaching, drawn from research and from what the most effective teachers do.

    Rosenshine

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Start with a short review, present new material in small steps, ask many questions, and check understanding before students practise alone. Aim for a high success rate in guided practice before you let go.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft the daily review questions and the checks for understanding, which saves time on the parts of the lesson that are easy to skip.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Students who get answers from AI during independent practice show a high success rate that is not theirs, so you move on too early.
    The evidence
    Rosenshine's principles summarise the wider research on explicit instruction for classroom use (Rosenshine 2012). They are a synthesis of strong evidence, never tested as a package. A meta-analysis of 328 studies found consistently positive effects of Direct Instruction, a highly structured form of explicit teaching (Stockard et al. 2018).

    Source: Rosenshine 2012, American Educator, Stockard et al. 2018, Review of Educational Research

    Desirable difficulties FrameworkSome kinds of harder practice feel worse but make learning last longer: spacing, mixing and testing yourself.

    Bjork

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Swap one re-read for a quiz from memory, spread practice over several lessons, and mix problem types in a set. Warn students that it will feel harder and that this is the point.
    Where AI helps
    AI can quickly make mixed, spaced practice sets that pull in content from earlier weeks, which is tedious to do by hand.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI removes exactly the useful struggle: a student who asks for the answer the moment a question feels hard skips the effort that makes it stick.
    The evidence
    Desirable difficulties is an umbrella idea, not a single tested method (Bjork & Bjork 2020). Two of the strategies under it, practice testing and distributed practice, are rated high utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Mixing problem types helps most when students must tell similar things apart (Brunmair & Richter 2019). A difficulty is only desirable if students can meet it.

    Source: Bjork & Bjork 2020, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Brunmair & Richter 2019, Psychological Bulletin

    ICAP FrameworkSorts tasks by how students engage: passive, active, constructive, interactive. Higher up tends to mean deeper learning.

    Chi & Wylie

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Look at one task and ask what students actually do. Highlighting is active; explaining in their own words is constructive; building on a partner's explanation is interactive. Upgrade one passive or active step.
    Where AI helps
    ICAP is a sharp lens for AI tasks: ask whether students generate something themselves or only read what the tool generated, and redesign toward the first.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Reading an AI explanation is passive even when it feels like work, so a lesson full of AI chats can look interactive while students only consume.
    The evidence
    Chi & Wylie (2014) review studies that mostly fit the order interactive > constructive > active > passive. It is a framework for judging tasks, not a method tested as one intervention, and the level depends on what students actually do, not on the task label.

    Source: Chi & Wylie 2014, Educational Psychologist

    Variation theory FrameworkStudents notice what matters when one thing varies while everything else stays the same.

    Marton

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    To show what a concept is, keep everything fixed except the feature you want students to see. Put two examples side by side that differ in just one way, and ask what changed and what stayed the same.
    Where AI helps
    AI can generate sets of examples that differ in exactly one feature, which you then check for accuracy and use as contrast pairs.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI-made examples often vary in several ways at once, so the feature students should notice gets lost among the differences.
    The evidence
    Variation theory comes from Marton's research in Gothenburg (Marton 2014). The learning-study tradition built on it reports results as case studies, not pooled effects. A meta-analysis of the mechanism behind it, learning by comparing cases, found a positive effect across 57 experiments (Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013).

    Source: Marton 2014, Necessary Conditions of Learning (Routledge), Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn 2013, Educational Psychologist

    Motivation theories FrameworkStudents engage more when they feel some choice, can succeed and belong (SDT), and when they expect success and value the task.

    Ryan & Deci; Eccles

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Give a real choice within the task, set challenge that students can meet, and say why the task matters. Ask yourself which is missing for a reluctant class: choice, success, belonging or value.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest several versions of a task with different choices or real-world links, so students can pick one without you writing them all.
    Where AI gets in the way
    When AI does the hard part, students lose the feeling of having succeeded themselves, which is what builds competence and motivation.
    The evidence
    Self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci 2000) and expectancy-value theory (Eccles & Wigfield 2002) are well supported as theories of motivation. Here they are used as lenses for planning, not as tested classroom methods.

    Source: Ryan & Deci 2000, Contemporary Educational Psychology, Eccles & Wigfield 2002, Annual Review of Psychology

    Visible Learning FrameworkHattie's synthesis of hundreds of meta-analyses, ranking what affects achievement. A useful map with contested rankings.

    Hattie

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Use it as a map of what research has looked at, then check the strand you care about in a careful source such as the EEF Toolkit. Never pick a method because of its place in a ranking.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you trace a Visible Learning effect size back to the studies behind it, so you can see what was actually measured and with whom.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI tools often repeat effect-size league tables as settled fact, which makes a contested ranking sound more certain than it is.
    The evidence
    Visible Learning brings together a large body of meta-analyses (Hattie 2008). It is a useful map, but its effect-size rankings are contested: effect sizes from different kinds of studies cannot be compared or ranked in this way (Simpson 2018). The EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit is a safer anchor.

    Source: Hattie 2008, Visible Learning (Routledge), Simpson 2018, British Educational Research Journal, EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit

    Kolb's learning cycle FrameworkLearning as a cycle: experience something, reflect on it, form an idea, then test it in a new situation.

    Kolb

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    After a practical or field trip, do not stop at the experience. Ask what happened, what explains it, and where students could try the idea next. Use the cycle; skip the learning style inventory.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft reflection and transfer questions for a specific practical, so the cycle continues after the hands-on part.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI writes the reflection, students skip the step where experience turns into an idea, and the cycle stops at doing.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis of 89 studies found experiential teaching beat traditional instruction on average (Burch et al. 2019), but it does not isolate Kolb's four-stage cycle, and the studies' education level is not reported. Kolb's learning style inventory is a different thing: matching teaching to learning styles is not supported (Pashler et al. 2008).

    Source: Burch, Giambatista, Batchelor, Burch, Hoover & Heller 2019, Decision Sciences Journal of Innovative Education, Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest

    Information processing model FrameworkA map of memory: information passes through a small working memory and is stored in a large long-term memory.

    Atkinson & Shiffrin; Baddeley

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    When a student struggles, ask where it breaks: did they notice the information, could they hold it while working, or can they not recall it later? Each answer points to a different fix.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you split a dense explanation into smaller steps, so less has to be held in working memory at once.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Long AI answers overload working memory, and students who never have to recall anything store little in long-term memory.
    The evidence
    The model goes back to Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968), and Baddeley's working memory model (2000) has refined it. It is a simplified map that cognitive load theory builds on, not a method tested as one intervention.

    Source: Atkinson & Shiffrin 1968, Psychology of Learning and Motivation, Baddeley 2000, Trends in Cognitive Sciences

    Executive functions FrameworkThe mental skills that steer learning: working memory, self-control and flexible thinking. They keep developing through the teens.

    Diamond

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Be the external executive function: break tasks into steps, show the plan on the board, give checklists and time cues. Then hand more of the planning to students as they cope. Skip brain-training programmes.
    Where AI helps
    AI can turn a big assignment into a step-by-step checklist with time estimates, which helps students who struggle to plan.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI always plans and organises the work, students do not practise planning themselves, and the support never fades.
    The evidence
    Executive functions are a valid, well-studied construct (Diamond 2013). Training programmes are contested: a meta-analysis found working memory training improves trained tasks but does not transfer to other skills or school learning (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016).

    Source: Diamond 2013, Annual Review of Psychology, Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science

    Neuroplasticity FrameworkThe brain changes with learning: practice strengthens the networks used, and unused connections weaken.

    Neuroscience

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the message, not a method: ability grows with practice, so build in repeated, spaced practice and tell students why it works. Practice strengthens the networks involved, partly through more connections and possibly more myelination of the pathways used.
    Where AI helps
    AI can generate the many varied practice questions that repeated, spaced practice needs.
    Where AI gets in the way
    The brain changes through what students do themselves, so practice that AI does for them builds nothing.
    The evidence
    Brain imaging shows learning changes both grey and white matter (Zatorre, Fields & Johansen-Berg 2012). A correction: myelin wraps axons, not synapses, so repetition does not "myelinate synapses". Neuroscience gives no direct classroom prescription, and many brain claims in education are myths (Howard-Jones 2014).

    Source: Zatorre, Fields & Johansen-Berg 2012, Nature Neuroscience, Howard-Jones 2014, Nature Reviews Neuroscience

    The social brain FrameworkThe brain is tuned to other people. Feeling safe and accepted frees attention for learning, especially in the teens.

    Cozolino; Lieberman

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Make it safe to be wrong: answer mistakes calmly, use mini whiteboards so everyone answers and no one is exposed, and give everyone a role in group work. Learn names and notice who is left out.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft discussion roles and sentence starters that give quieter students a safe way into group talk.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Students who ask a chatbot instead of a classmate or teacher lose the relationships and belonging that support learning.
    The evidence
    Adolescence may be a sensitive period for social processing (Blakemore & Mills 2014), and stress impairs the prefrontal functions learning depends on (Arnsten 2009). This is background science with little direct classroom testing. Belonging is linked to motivation and achievement in secondary school (Korpershoek et al. 2020).

    Source: Blakemore & Mills 2014, Annual Review of Psychology, Arnsten 2009, Nature Reviews Neuroscience, Korpershoek et al. 2020, Research Papers in Education

    D Theory Theory

    How does it explain learning? Theories and philosophies that shape values and choices.

    Behaviorism TheoryLearning is a change in behavior, shaped by practice, feedback and reinforcement.

    Skinner; Watson

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the clear parts: say exactly what students should be able to do, break it into small steps, and give quick feedback on each attempt. Praise specific effort and progress, and keep routines predictable so students know what success looks like.
    Where AI helps
    AI can turn one learning goal into a ladder of short practice items with instant, specific feedback, so every student gets more correct repetitions in a lesson.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Points, streaks and badges in AI apps can make students chase the reward instead of the understanding. When the reward stops, so does the effort.
    The evidence
    Behaviorism is a theory of learning, not one method, so it cannot be tested as a single intervention (Skinner 1958). Its legacy lives on in methods that have been tested, such as highly structured explicit teaching with frequent practice and feedback (Stockard et al. 2018).

    Source: Skinner 1958, Science, Stockard et al. 2018, Review of Educational Research

    Cognitivism TheoryThe mind actively takes in, organizes and stores information, and working memory can only hold a little at a time.

    Piaget; Miller

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow its attention to how the mind handles new material: start from what students already know, go from simple to complex, and present one idea at a time. Check what they actually remember before you build on it.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you split a dense text or explanation into smaller chunks and suggest which ideas students must know first.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI produces long, cluttered slides or explanations, it overloads the same limited working memory the theory warns about.
    The evidence
    Cognitivism is a theory, not a single intervention. Early work showed how little we can hold in mind at once (Miller 1956), and cognitive load theory, which grew from this tradition, has decades of replicated experiments behind it (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019). "The brain works like a computer" is a metaphor from the theory, not a research finding.

    Source: Miller 1956, Psychological Review, Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019, Educational Psychology Review

    Constructivism TheoryStudents build their own understanding by connecting new ideas to what they already know.

    Piaget; Dewey

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the starting point: find out what students already think, including their misconceptions, and design tasks that make them use and revise that thinking. Guide them closely while they explore, with worked examples, prompts and feedback.
    Where AI helps
    AI can draft a list of common misconceptions about a topic and a short diagnostic question for each, so you see what students bring before you teach.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If students ask AI for the finished explanation, they skip the thinking that builds understanding. The answer looks constructed, but they did not construct it.
    The evidence
    Constructivism is an account of how learning happens, not a teaching method. It does not mean minimal guidance: novices learn less when left to discover on their own (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006). A meta-analysis found that unassisted discovery did not help, while discovery with guidance, feedback or worked examples did (Alfieri et al. 2011).

    Source: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, Educational Psychologist, Alfieri et al. 2011, Journal of Educational Psychology

    Sociocultural theory TheoryLearning happens through language and interaction, with support just beyond what a student can do alone.

    Vygotsky

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow scaffolding: aim tasks just beyond what students can do alone, support them with talk, models and prompts, then remove the support step by step. Plan structured talk so students explain their thinking to each other.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you write three levels of support for the same task, from full sentence starters to a single hint, so you can fade the scaffold as students grow.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI can become a scaffold that never comes down. If a student always works with the chatbot's help, the task never moves into what they can do alone.
    The evidence
    Sociocultural theory explains learning; it is not a single intervention (Vygotsky 1978). Scaffolding, the classroom idea that grew from it (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976), is widely described, but a review of a decade of research found its effects are hard to isolate and seldom measured (van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen 2010).

    Source: Vygotsky 1978, Mind in Society, Harvard University Press, Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976, Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen 2010, Educational Psychology Review

    Progressivism TheoryLearning by doing: school should connect to students' lives and prepare them to take part in a democracy.

    Dewey

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow real purpose: tie a unit to a genuine question or problem, and give students real choices within it. Keep the subject content explicit and guide the work, so the doing leads to learning.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest real-world problems or local cases that fit your curriculum goals, which you then check and adapt.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI makes it easy to produce a polished project without the learning. Students can deliver the poster or the report and skip the doing that was the point.
    The evidence
    Progressivism is a philosophy of education, not a single method, so it cannot be tested as one (Dewey 1916). Its descendants, such as project and inquiry work, depend heavily on how much guidance students get; novices learn less with minimal guidance (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006).

    Source: Dewey 1916, Democracy and Education (Project Gutenberg), Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, Educational Psychologist

    Critical pedagogy TheoryEducation is never neutral: students learn to question power and act on the world through dialogue.

    Freire

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the questions: whose voice is in this text, whose is missing, and who benefits? Use dialogue where students' own experiences count as material, not just the teacher's answers.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you find several perspectives on the same event or issue, so students can compare whose story gets told.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI answers often sound neutral but carry the biases of their training data. If students treat them as the objective view, they learn the opposite of what this tradition aims for.
    The evidence
    Critical pedagogy is a philosophy about the purpose of education, not a method with outcome studies (Freire 1970). It shapes values and choices, but there is no controlled evidence on it as a single intervention.

    Source: Freire 1970, Pedagogy of the Oppressed (excerpt), Rowman & Littlefield 2008

    Humanistic perspective TheoryLearning is personal growth: students learn best when they feel safe, respected and seen as whole people.

    Rogers; Maslow

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the relationship: greet students, learn what matters to them, and give them real voice in how they work. Make it safe to be wrong, while keeping expectations high.
    Where AI helps
    AI can save you time on routine paperwork, which you can give back to students as conversations and feedback.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI writes your comments to students, they can feel generic and impersonal. Students notice when no one actually read their work.
    The evidence
    The humanistic perspective is a theory of motivation and growth, not a single intervention (Maslow 1943). A meta-analysis of mostly correlational studies found that warm, learner-centered teacher-student relationships go with better engagement and achievement (Cornelius-White 2007).

    Source: Maslow 1943, Psychological Review, Cornelius-White 2007, Review of Educational Research

    E School model School model

    What kind of school is it? Whole-school movements.

    Montessori School modelStudents work independently in a prepared environment, choosing tasks and working at their own pace.

    Maria Montessori

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the prepared environment: clear stations or task cards, materials students can use without asking, and routines that let them work on their own. Keep the teacher's role active: observe, then step in with the next challenge.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you write clear self-check cards for independent tasks, so students know when they are done and what to do next.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Working at one's own pace with AI can turn into working alone with a chatbot. Montessori depends on an adult who observes and steps in; AI does not replace that.
    The evidence
    Lottery-based studies, where families applied and places were drawn at random, show positive results for public Montessori preschool (Lillard et al. 2017; Lillard et al. 2025, 588 children). This evidence covers preschool only; we found no controlled evidence for Grades 6–12.

    Source: Lillard et al. 2017, Frontiers in Psychology, Lillard et al. 2025, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

    Waldorf (Steiner) education School modelHead, heart and hands: Steiner's model balances academic, artistic and practical work in set developmental stages.

    Rudolf Steiner

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the balance: let students draw, build, tell or perform as a way into academic content, not as decoration. Oral storytelling can open a topic before students read about it.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest practical or artistic tasks that fit a specific learning goal, which you then judge for whether they lead to the content.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI-generated images and texts can replace the handwork and craft that this tradition values. A student who prompts a picture has not practiced drawing.
    The evidence
    We found no rigorous controlled studies of Waldorf outcomes. Stehlik 2019 reviews the research literature on Steiner Waldorf education.

    Source: Stehlik 2019, Waldorf Schools and the History of Steiner Education, Springer

    Reggio Emilia School modelA preschool philosophy that sees children as capable, learns through projects, and makes learning visible through documentation.

    Loris Malaguzzi

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow documentation: collect photos, quotes and drafts from a project and put them in front of students to discuss how their thinking changed. Use their questions to shape the next step.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you sort and summarize notes from a project, so you spot patterns in students' thinking faster.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI summaries can flatten what students actually said. Documentation only works when the teacher looks closely at the real work.
    The evidence
    Reggio Emilia is a preschool approach with little controlled evidence. A quasi-experimental evaluation found long-run benefits compared with no formal childcare, but small and insignificant differences compared with other formal childcare (Biroli et al. 2018).

    Source: Biroli et al. 2018, Research in Economics

    Freinet School modelStudents learn through meaningful work for a real audience, like printing their own school newspaper.

    Célestin Freinet

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Borrow the real audience: let students write, record or present for readers outside the classroom, such as another class, parents or the local community. Knowing someone will read it raises the bar for revision.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help students check a draft for clarity before it goes to a real reader, as long as they decide what to change.
    Where AI gets in the way
    If AI writes the article, the real audience reads the machine, not the student. The point of the audience disappears.
    The evidence
    We found little controlled evidence on Freinet pedagogy as a whole (Legrand 1993 gives an overview). Its idea of a real audience for student work lives on in many classrooms.

    Source: Legrand 1993, Prospects

    Combination recipes

    Models work best together. Three lesson shapes that combine strong evidence.

    The knowledge lesson

    • Retrieval practice Strong
    • Explicit instruction Strong
    • Cognitive load and worked examples Strong
    1. Open with three retrieval questions from earlier lessons (5 min).
    2. Explain and model the new idea in small steps, with one worked example (15 min).
    3. Guided practice with a half-finished example, checking as you go (10 min).
    4. Independent practice, then one exit question on today's goal (15 min).

    The discussion lesson

    • Formative assessment Strong
    • Collaborative learning Strong
    • Dialogic teaching Promising
    1. One open question that has more than one good answer.
    2. Think alone first (2 min), then talk in pairs (5 min).
    3. Whole-class discussion where students build on each other's answers (15 min).
    4. A hinge question at the end shows whether the key idea has landed (5 min).

    The practice lesson

    • Retrieval practice Strong
    • Feedback Strong
    • Interleaving Promising
    1. A short quiz from memory, mixing this week and earlier weeks (8 min).
    2. Students check their own answers against a key and mark what they missed (5 min).
    3. Mixed practice where students must choose the method, not just apply it (20 min).
    4. Feedback on one thing to do differently next time (10 min).

    F Myth check

    Ideas many teachers believe in, which the research does not support as teaching methods. Each card says what to do instead.

    Learning styles (VAK) ContestedThe belief that students learn better when teaching matches their style, such as visual, auditory or kinesthetic.

    Various

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Instead of sorting students by style, match the format to the content: diagrams for structures, spoken explanation for stories, hands-on work for skills. Combining words and pictures helps all students.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you turn a text explanation into a clear diagram plus a short spoken version, chosen for the content rather than for a student's supposed style.
    Where AI gets in the way
    Many AI tools still offer to adapt material to a student's "learning style". Following that advice spends your time on something that does not work.
    The evidence
    A major review found no adequate evidence that matching teaching to a learning style improves learning (Pashler et al. 2008). The myth is still widespread: 93% of UK and 96% of Dutch teachers surveyed believed it (Dekker et al. 2012).

    Source: Pashler et al. 2008, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Dekker et al. 2012, Frontiers in Psychology

    Multiple intelligences as a teaching method ContestedGardner's idea of several separate intelligences, used as a method to teach to each student's strongest one.

    Gardner

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Instead of teaching to "intelligences", plan for the content and let students show what they have learned in more than one way when that fits the goal. Variety is fine; labeling students is not.
    Where AI helps
    AI can suggest several ways for students to show the same learning goal, such as a written answer, a diagram or a short oral explanation, which you check against the goal.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI can quickly produce eight versions of a lesson, one per intelligence. That multiplies your planning without evidence it improves learning.
    The evidence
    A critical review concluded that multiple intelligences theory lacks adequate empirical support (Waterhouse 2006). Giving students varied ways to show learning is fine; teaching to separate "intelligences" is not supported.

    Source: Waterhouse 2006, Educational Psychologist

    Brain and working-memory training ContestedComputer games and drills meant to boost working memory or general brain power, with gains expected to spread to school learning.

    Various commercial programmes

    On an ordinary Tuesday
    Instead of training working memory in isolation, reduce the load in your lessons: smaller steps, worked examples, and clear written instructions students can look back at. Practice the actual subject skills you want to improve.
    Where AI helps
    AI can help you rewrite long instructions as a short numbered checklist, which takes pressure off working memory during the task.
    Where AI gets in the way
    AI-powered "brain games" are easy to find and look scientific. Time spent on them is time not spent practicing subject content.
    The evidence
    A meta-analysis found that working-memory training improves performance on tasks like the ones trained, but does not transfer to intelligence or school skills such as reading and arithmetic (Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016).

    Source: Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme 2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science

    AI tools and age rules

    You use AI to plan. If students use AI, these rules apply (October 2026):

    • Copilot Chat: Students aged 13 and over, through the school's own Microsoft 365 account. The school's administrator must set each student's age group and consent.
    • Gemini for Education: Open to education users of all ages, with a stricter experience for under-18s. The school's administrator must turn it on.
