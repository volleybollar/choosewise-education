    The model map

    About fifty models in six categories. The categories answer different questions: does it work in classrooms, does it help you plan, how does it explain learning, what kind of school is it, and should teachers stop believing it.

    • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that teaching this way improves learning.
    • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
    • Framework A useful structure for planning, with no intervention studies behind it as a method.
    • Theory A way of explaining how learning happens. It shapes values and choices, but cannot be tested as a single intervention.
    • School model A whole-school movement with its own philosophy and practice.
    • Contested The research does not support using it as a teaching method, even though many teachers believe in it.

    Combination recipes

    Models work best together. Three lesson shapes that combine strong evidence.

    The knowledge lesson

    • Retrieval practice Strong
    • Explicit instruction Strong
    • Cognitive load and worked examples Strong
    1. Open with three retrieval questions from earlier lessons (5 min).
    2. Explain and model the new idea in small steps, with one worked example (15 min).
    3. Guided practice with a half-finished example, checking as you go (10 min).
    4. Independent practice, then one exit question on today's goal (15 min).

    The discussion lesson

    • Formative assessment Strong
    • Collaborative learning Strong
    • Dialogic teaching Promising
    1. One open question that has more than one good answer.
    2. Think alone first (2 min), then talk in pairs (5 min).
    3. Whole-class discussion where students build on each other's answers (15 min).
    4. A hinge question at the end shows whether the key idea has landed (5 min).

    The practice lesson

    • Retrieval practice Strong
    • Feedback Strong
    • Interleaving Promising
    1. A short quiz from memory, mixing this week and earlier weeks (8 min).
    2. Students check their own answers against a key and mark what they missed (5 min).
    3. Mixed practice where students must choose the method, not just apply it (20 min).
    4. Feedback on one thing to do differently next time (10 min).

    AI tools and age rules

    You use AI to plan. If students use AI, these rules apply (October 2026):

    • Copilot Chat: Students aged 13 and over, through the school's own Microsoft 365 account. The school's administrator must set each student's age group and consent.
    • Gemini for Education: Open to education users of all ages, with a stricter experience for under-18s. The school's administrator must turn it on.
    • ChatGPT: Ages 13 and over on personal accounts, with a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. ChatGPT for Teachers is for school staff only.
    • Claude: Ages 18 and over. In a school, for teachers only.

    Rules differ between countries and change often. Check your own national rules and your school policy before students use any AI tool.
