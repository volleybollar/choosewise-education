Unit Studio

Take a unit you have already planned, or one you are about to plan, through five research steps with your own AI tool. You end up with an improved plan, and with a method you can use on every unit after this one.

  • No personal data. The prompts hold your unit, never student names or results.
  • Any AI tool. Copilot, Gemini, ChatGPT or Claude.
  • At your own pace. One step at a time, today or next week.

What a step looks like

Forces and motion · Grade 8 science, 4 weeks

The unit as planned

Week 1: What is a force? Pushes and pulls, contact and non-contact forces. Video and notes. Worksheet. Week 2: Measuring forces with newtonmeters (practical). Weight and mass. Notes. Week 3: Balanced and unbalanced forces. Textbook reading and questions. Group poster. Week 4: Speed = distance ÷ time, practical with toy cars. Revision lesson. End-of-unit test.

Step 4: retrieval and checkpoints

Keep
The practicals in weeks 2 and 4 give students something real to measure, and the end-of-unit test gives you evidence of what they learned.
Suggest
1. Start every lesson from week 2 with three retrieval questions from earlier weeks, for example: Is gravity a contact or a non-contact force? What is the difference between mass and weight? Why this helps: recalling content again over several weeks makes it last far longer than reading the notes again. 2. Add a hinge question at the end of week 3, before speed: “A car moves at a steady speed in a straight line. Are the forces on it balanced or unbalanced?” The common wrong answer is unbalanced, because it is moving. Move on if about 4 in 5 answer balanced. Why this helps: it shows whether the class has the key idea before you build on it. 3. Replace the revision lesson with a retrieval lesson: a mixed quiz from all four weeks, marked by the students, followed by re-teaching of the two most-missed questions. Why this helps: testing yourself helps memory more than re-reading.
Question
Which idea from week 1 or 2 have students most often forgotten by the end-of-unit test?

How it works

  1. Start. Paste a unit you have planned, or describe the topic and goals of a new one.
  2. Five steps. Each step gives you a prompt. The AI looks at your unit through that step and suggests at most three changes, each with why it helps students learn. You decide what to keep.
  3. The improved plan. The last prompt asks the AI to write the whole plan with your decisions. You review it before you teach it.

Work through the steps in one AI conversation if you can: the AI then remembers your unit. Every prompt also works on its own, so you can stop and come back another day. Paste what you have decided so far into "Decisions so far".

Prior knowledge

New learning builds on what students already know. A gap found in the first lesson costs five minutes; found in the test, it costs the unit.

Step 3

Sequence and load

Working memory is small. New ideas land better when they are explained, modelled and practised in small steps.

Look for

  • How much new does each lesson bring?
  • Are new ideas modelled with worked examples before students practise alone?
  • Where does support fade as students get more confident?

The improved plan · Forces and motion

Goal and evidence
By the end, students can explain forces as pushes and pulls and name contact and non-contact forces, measure forces in newtons and tell weight from mass, predict motion from balanced and unbalanced forces, and calculate speed from distance and time. Evidence: the exit ticket in lesson 3, the hinge questions in lessons 6 and 9, and the end-of-unit test, which asks students to explain as well as calculate.
Prior knowledge
Students need to read a scale, divide with decimals and know that a dropped object falls because of gravity. Check it in lesson 1 with three quick questions: read a scale, work out 12 ÷ 4.8, and explain why a dropped ball falls.
Lessons
1. Prior knowledge check (5 min); worked examples of contact and non-contact forces, then sorting cards in pairs (50 min) 2. Retrieval (5 min); force arrows modelled on the board, then students draw arrows for everyday situations (50 min) 3. Retrieval (5 min); mixed practice and an exit ticket on contact and non-contact forces (50 min) 4. Retrieval (5 min); a worked example with a newtonmeter, then the measuring practical (55 min) 5. Retrieval (5 min); weight and mass: a worked example of weight = mass × 10 N/kg, then guided practice (50 min) 6. Retrieval (5 min); mixed practice on weight and mass, ending with a hinge question (50 min) 7. Retrieval (5 min); balanced and unbalanced forces: demonstration and worked examples with force arrows (50 min) 8. Retrieval (5 min); textbook reading with three guiding questions, then a group poster explaining one situation (50 min) 9. Retrieval (5 min); hinge question on steady speed, re-teaching if needed, then practice (50 min) 10. Retrieval (5 min); speed = distance ÷ time: worked examples, then the toy car practical (55 min) 11. A mixed retrieval quiz from all four weeks, marked by students, then re-teaching of the two most-missed questions and speed practice from half-finished examples (55 min) 12. End-of-unit test (55 min)
Retrieval
Lessons 2 to 11 open with three questions: one from the last lesson, one from one to two weeks back and one older. Lesson 11 is a mixed quiz from all four weeks.
Checkpoints
Lesson 3: exit ticket on contact and non-contact forces. Lesson 6: hinge question on mass and weight. Lesson 9: hinge question on steady speed. If fewer than about 4 in 5 get it, re-teach with a new worked example at the start of the next lesson before moving on.
AI use
Students do not use AI for the practicals, the force arrows or the calculations: they would get worse at explaining and measuring forces themselves. The teacher uses AI to write the retrieval questions and hinge questions. In lesson 8, students may ask an AI tool to point out one physics mistake in their poster explanation after writing it themselves, if the school allows students to use AI.
Changes
The video and notes in week 1 are replaced by worked examples and sorting. Retrieval questions open lessons 2 to 11. An exit ticket in lesson 3 and hinge questions in lessons 6 and 9 are added. The revision lesson becomes a mixed retrieval quiz. Speed calculations fade from worked examples to independent work.
Check
Which value of g your syllabus uses (10 N/kg or 9.8 N/kg). Whether the toy car practical fits one lesson with your equipment.

Why it works

  • Strong Meta-analyses of classroom studies show that teaching this way improves learning.
  • Promising Controlled studies show gains, but results are mixed, small in number or dependent on context.
  • Framework A useful structure for planning, with no intervention studies behind it as a method.
  • Choosewise Choosewise's own question, not a research finding.

Backward design and constructive alignment Framework

Backward design starts from what students should be able to do and how that will show, and plans the activities last (Wiggins & McTighe 2005). Constructive alignment makes the same point: goals, activities and assessment should match (Biggs 1996). Both are frameworks for planning, not methods tested in intervention studies.

Source: Wiggins & McTighe 2005, Understanding by Design (ASCD), Biggs 1996, Higher Education

Prior knowledge Promising

What students already know is one of the strongest predictors of how well they do later. A meta-analysis finds that prior knowledge strongly predicts later performance, but explains much less of how much students gain (Simonsmeier et al. 2022). Checking prior knowledge early lets the teacher fill gaps before building on them.

Source: Simonsmeier, Flaig, Deiglmayr, Schalk & Schneider 2022, Educational Psychologist

Explicit instruction and worked examples Strong

A meta-analysis of 328 studies over half a century found consistently positive effects of Direct Instruction, a highly structured form of explicit teaching (Stockard et al. 2018). Rosenshine's principles summarise the wider research on explicit instruction for classroom use. Cognitive load theory rests on decades of replicated experiments. The worked-example effect is one of its most robust findings, confirmed in a 2023 meta-analysis, and it fades as students gain expertise (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; Barbieri et al. 2023).

Source: Stockard et al. 2018, Review of Educational Research, Rosenshine 2012, American Educator, Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019, Educational Psychology Review, Barbieri et al. 2023, Educational Psychology Review

Retrieval practice and spacing Strong

Practice testing and distributed practice are the two strategies rated high utility in the best-known review of study techniques (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Meta-analyses of classroom studies confirm that retrieval practice improves learning in school (Yang et al. 2021; Ruitenburg et al. 2026), and find a small but reliable benefit from spacing practice out (Ruitenburg et al. 2026).

Source: Dunlosky et al. 2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, Yang et al. 2021, Psychological Bulletin, Ruitenburg et al. 2026, Educational Psychology Review

Checkpoints (formative assessment) Strong

Black & Wiliam's 1998 review made the case that formative assessment raises achievement, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 118 K-12 studies confirms a positive, more modest average effect (Yao et al. 2024). A whole-school EEF trial found a similar, real effect at scale.

Source: Black & Wiliam 1998, Assessment in Education, Yao et al. 2024, Educational Research and Evaluation, EEF: Embedding Formative Assessment evaluation

Belonging and motivation Promising

A meta-analytic review links school belonging to motivation, well-being and achievement in secondary school (Korpershoek et al. 2020). Brief belonging interventions have helped some students, but a 2023 study of almost 27,000 students found they worked only where the school gave students real chances to belong (Walton et al. 2023).

Source: Korpershoek et al. 2020, Research Papers in Education, Walton & Cohen 2011, Science, Walton et al. 2023, Science

The trade-off question Choosewise

This is Choosewise's own question, not a research finding. It draws on research showing that unrestricted AI help can raise practice scores while lowering learning once the help is removed (Bastani et al. 2025).

Source: Bastani et al. 2025, PNAS

AI tools and age rules

You use AI to plan. If students use AI in the unit, these rules apply (October 2026):

  • Copilot Chat: Students aged 13 and over, through the school's own Microsoft 365 account. The school's administrator must set each student's age group and consent.
  • Gemini for Education: Open to education users of all ages, with a stricter experience for under-18s. The school's administrator must turn it on.
  • ChatGPT: Ages 13 and over on personal accounts, with a parent's or guardian's permission under 18. ChatGPT for Teachers is for school staff only.
  • Claude: Ages 18 and over. In a school, for teachers only.

Rules differ between countries and change often. Check your own national rules and your school policy before students use any AI tool.
